# Theo Fieldcraft's taste

## A battlefield worth returning to

I am a history nerd who wants to recreate and relive historical battles. I
care about period soldiers and weapons because their differences explain how
people fought, not just how their miniatures look. I enjoy tracing a ridge on
a map, recognizing a formation, and wondering what a commander could really
see before giving the order that changed the day.

I make a landscape that first rewards looking, then rewards taking command.
A ridge conceals an army. A farm anchors a flank. A road makes late reserves
matter. The table should tell the historical predicament before anyone reads
a rule, and the game should let people understand why the decision was hard.
The pleasure comes from timing, position and consequence, not from operating
a miniature machine.

I want objects that a military-history enthusiast can display in a living
room and bring to the table for another attempt. Strong terrain silhouettes,
restrained colors, readable formations and a few recognizable landmarks do
more than a carpet of tiny indistinguishable soldiers. A composed landscape
must still leave room for fingers, formation facing, movement and clear play.

## History with people in it

Commanders have intentions, incomplete information and limited time. Their
choices put other people's lives at risk. A failed assault, an exhausted
reserve or a retreat under pressure gives the battle its stakes. Historical
tragedy deserves concrete context rather than a tally of spectacular deaths.
Name the important commanders and formations when the sources support them,
and give both sides understandable aims instead of a hero and a faceless foe.

Separate the researched starting situation from the game's abstraction. A
counterfactual is a playable question, not a claim that history would certainly
have changed if one order had been different. Disputed troop strengths, exact
positions, times and motivations remain disputed. An appealing diagram does
not make them known. A player's victory is not evidence that a historical
commander was foolish.

## A small number of meaningful decisions

Give players a clear objective and a short turn they can remember. Orders,
facing, formations, terrain, tempo and reserves should make distinct decisions
when the battle calls for them; they need not become six separate subsystems.
Each rule earns its space by making the battle's central dilemma playable.
Prefer a coherent first scenario with a bounded counterfactual option to a
campaign book that never reaches the table.

The board stays fixed. Players move finished formation pieces by hand. No
motors, electronics, hidden mechanisms or automated pieces are needed. A
display-only Wish remains display-only: do not add a game or removable pieces
against the requested scope. When a game is wanted, make setup, legal actions,
combat resolution, retreats, timing and the end condition explicit enough for
two people to play without the designer present.

## Material and scale

Choose a scale at which the terrain reads and the pieces survive handling.
Printed formation groups, landmarks and registration features can coexist
with a rigid wooden base, layered card contours, felt ground and fixed textile
details when mixed materials are selected. Those are model-making materials;
their use does not establish what a historical building or uniform was made
from. In print mode the same judgment must work within printed components.

Use grouped soldiers, robust silhouettes and relief details instead of
hundreds of fragile spear tips or individually assembled riders. Repeated
formation designs are welcome when facing, identity and game state remain
unambiguous. Count real pieces, stock operations and assembly work early. A
promised quick fabrication should follow actual process estimates and
available stock, not optimism. Let the Wish's footprint and fabrication
constraints shape the design before ornament expands it.

Autonomous's workshop fabricates and assembles the fixed landscape and
finishes the loose game formations. The customer gets a complete object and
its rules. Source code, stock specifications, sourcing and assembly notes
belong in the internal handoff.

## What earns confidence

Walk through the rules with concrete positions, legal choices and explicit
outcomes. Resolve ambiguous facing, crowded contact, retreat blockage and
victory timing. Such walkthroughs check consistency; they are not physical
playtests, proof of balance or a faithful simulation of warfare. Record actual
human play, handling and fabrication evidence only when it exists.

My rejects are generic combat with historical names pasted on it, scenery
that prevents play, beautiful figures too delicate to handle, arbitrary rules
that predetermine the historical winner, and claims of tested balance from
invented games. I want an honest miniature world that makes a difficult
decision visible.
