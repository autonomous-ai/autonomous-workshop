# Product manual visual system

Use this as a composition kit, not a fixed template. The toy's Wish, form,
interaction, and Inventor Taste still choose the imagery, voice, palette, type,
and physical format.

## What consistently works

1. **One emotional promise.** Give the cover a short phrase that names the
   experience, not the manufacturing category. Let one exact product render
   dominate. Add only the minimum ownership or setup cue needed before opening.
2. **One coherent visual language.** Choose one paper/background color, one
   dark ink, one primary accent, and at most two supporting accents. Reuse a
   small type scale, corner radius, stroke weight, icon family, and spacing
   rhythm on every page.
3. **Actions before exposition.** Use short verb-led headings, numbered steps,
   and one physical action or decision per panel. Pair words with an exact crop,
   silhouette, arrow, state diagram, or part icon derived from the Made bytes.
4. **A teaching arc.** Move from promise and inventory to setup, a guided first
   success, complete operation or rules, recovery, and safe reset/pack-away.
   Alternate overview and detail so the owner never faces an uninterrupted
   wall of rules.
5. **Deliberate pacing.** A simple one-piece Spark toy can be an excellent
   two-sided owner card. A multi-part mechanism often needs a four-page fold.
   A game may need more pages, but each extra page must teach a distinct action,
   state, example, or edge case.
6. **A strong ending.** Finish with the reset pose, memorable rule, care cue, or
   invitation that returns the product to its ready state.

## Reject these failure modes in the first render

- default A4 report styling, stock serif type, or repeated word-processor
  headers and footers;
- ASCII art, generic clip art, or a title floating over empty space when exact
  product imagery exists;
- paragraphs where a numbered action, decision card, state pair, or diagram
  would teach faster;
- tiny safety copy used to compensate for an overfull page;
- decoration unrelated to the product's form or interaction;
- color as the only carrier of identity, order, state, or outcome;
- a beautiful cover followed by visually unstructured instruction pages;
- internal CAD, verification, publication, or process language presented to
  the owner.

## Fast composition sequence

1. Write the creative brief and teaching arc before layout code.
2. Select the exact cover render and any two or three instructional views.
3. Define page geometry, safe area, palette, typography, and reusable panel,
   badge, arrow, and footer primitives once.
4. Build the complete smallest format with real copy and visuals—no lorem ipsum
   and no placeholder boxes.
5. Render every page together in color and grayscale. Inspect at physical size.
6. Give that complete first render to the required independent visual editor.
7. Resolve its strongest concrete finding and the largest first-owner problem
   in one revision, then rerender the complete manual once.

The visual review is evidence about an exact artifact, never an aesthetic score
or permission to invent unsupported product behavior.
