# A battlefield to display and replay

## Recommendation

**Choose Waterloo, 18 June 1815, for the first product.** It offers the strongest overall fit to broad recognition, varied terrain, distinctive forces, commander drama and replay through different formations, approach directions and timing. The proposed product is a finished mixed-material landscape with hand-positioned miniature formations and a concise original command game. It should be attractive enough to remain on display between sessions.

This is a product-design judgment. There is no objective historical ranking of the “coolest” battle, and historical fascination does not establish willingness to buy this particular object. The comparison spans the ancient Mediterranean, East Asia, Europe, Africa and the Americas, from the second millennium BCE to the twentieth century. It prioritizes the relationship between a meaningful decision and something a person can see and manipulate on the table.

The strongest alternatives are **Austerlitz** for the economy and clarity of its tactical problem, **Sekigahara** for loyalty and commander tragedy, **Marengo** for a late reversal with a personal human cost, and **Rivoli** for dramatic terrain and rapid concentration against converging attacks. **Alesia** remains an excellent static engineering diorama, but its siege geometry is less suitable for freely changing formations and maneuver. Waterloo's wider recognition is an editorial assessment, not a measured international awareness ranking or a claim that everyone knows the battle.

## What the product must do

The fixed terrain needs to explain the battle at a glance: important heights, crossing points, settlements, defensible approaches and exposed routes. The figures must be complete finished play pieces that can be repositioned by hand. Permanent attachment of every figure would prevent reenactment, so only the landscape and its fixed architectural details should be permanently assembled.

The experience needs three levels. First, an owner can admire a coherent miniature landscape. Second, a historical deployment and short narrative explain what happened. Third, an original set of command rules permits different decisions and outcomes. A scenario card alone is insufficient for replay if it does not explain movement, orders, engagement, morale and an ending condition.

Formation, facing and tempo must have observable consequences. A choice to march quickly should compete with readiness; committing a reserve should sacrifice future flexibility; exposing a flank should matter. These are proposed design requirements, not a finished or tested game system. The rules need an appropriate level of abstraction and physical playtesting before any claim of balanced or historically convincing play.

Historical summaries below are sourced. The product assessments are editorial judgments.

| Battle | Historical strategic interest | Fit for this product |
|---|---|---|

| **Austerlitz, 1805** | Allied movement toward the French right, the struggle for the Pratzen Heights, reinforcement timing and counterattacks across several sectors. [^1][^2] | Best focused tactical alternative; particularly clear central terrain and two connected fronts. |

## Why Waterloo wins this brief

Waterloo joins a familiar historical name to a substantial tactical problem. Wellington defended a ridge with forward strongpoints; Napoleon needed to break the coalition position while Blücher's Prussian army approached. The outcome ended Napoleon's return to power. These are stakes that a short introduction can make understandable. [^22]

The landscape supplies different kinds of encounter. Hougoumont, La Haye Sainte and Papelotte were forward strongpoints; Plancenoit became important as the Prussians intervened. Open ground, enclosed compounds, village approaches and ridges can create different decisions on the same board. The Belgian battlefield account is useful here because it keeps the Prussian action connected to the main battle. [^31]

The troop types offer visible contrast and interacting roles. A historical sketch preserved by the UK National Archives explicitly identifies infantry squares and the cavalry that charged them. This supports representing formation as a physical arrangement rather than an invisible statistic. Infantry, mounted troops and gun crews should have different capabilities, with their precise relationships defined and tested in the original rules. [^33]

The drama unfolds through several opportunities. Brown University's collection timeline connects the fighting around the farms, repeated cavalry attacks, the fall of La Haye Sainte and Prussian intervention. A product can let players revisit separate decision points rather than replay one mandatory charge. Timings and orders in the game would remain abstractions. [^30]

The subject already has a strong physical-display tradition: William Siborne's nineteenth-century Waterloo model is held by the National Army Museum. Its interpretation was contested, and the museum explains how much of the Prussian presence was removed. This is evidence both of the battlefield's appeal as a model and of the need to avoid treating an attractive reconstruction as neutral history. It is not evidence of demand for a new commercial product. [^32]

The principal tradeoff is size. Waterloo contains more actions than the first object should attempt to represent in full detail. The design must preserve the ridge, the major strongpoints and the Prussian approach while reducing figure counts through representative formations. If the result is cramped or difficult to handle, a smaller selected sector with an explicitly bounded scenario is preferable to an unreadable whole battlefield.

## Proposed physical object

Working concept: **Waterloo, 1815** — a historical landscape for display and command.

Use a composed landscape that preserves Wellington's ridge, the forward farms, the main open approaches and the eastern area needed to represent Prussian intervention. Preserve geographical relationships even if terrain and figure scales differ. Declare that compression in the historical guide. A whole battlefield at miniature-figure scale would be impractically large; the first object should be an interpretive model with a clear scope. Begin with a roughly coffee-table-sized design study and choose the final footprint from formation handling and route clearances, not from an arbitrary small size.

The display should have a finished wooden perimeter, subtly layered ground, carefully modeled village silhouettes and restrained vegetation. The formations and commanders need distinct silhouettes and readable faction identity at normal viewing distance. Fields, orchards, hedges, farm compounds and roads can create variety without inventing mountains or a river gorge. A reference study of the 1815 terrain, buildings and uniforms must precede detailed design. Later monuments and present-day visitor features do not belong in the battle scene.

The first miniature vocabulary should include ordinary infantry, skirmishers, elite infantry, cavalry, artillery and named command pieces. Distinguish the armies and their historical contingents through researched uniforms and flags. Do not reduce Wellington's multinational army to generic British redcoats, omit the Prussians, or mix uniforms from different decades. Exact regimental selections remain design research, not a settled production list.

| Material / process | Proposed role | Reason to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Plywood, laser cutting or CNC | Structural base and stable terrain substructure | Broad, rigid surfaces and a finished furniture-like edge. |
| Carved rigid foam | Ridges, slopes and shaped terrain beneath the surface finish | Lightweight bulk for the landscape; select the stock and cutting process together. |
| Layered paperboard | Ground contours, field boundaries and scenic planes | Controlled thickness and efficient large-area fabrication. |
| Cork, limited felt and short scenic fibers | Exposed ground, fields, hedges and vegetation masses | Tactile contrast with the sharper manufactured miniatures. Keep movement areas low and stable; exact placement still needs workshop instructions. |
| Thin wood | Farm details, fences, gates and beams | Real wood surfaces where they contribute at the chosen scale. |
| 3D-printed plastic | Infantry groups, cavalry, artillery, commanders, village details and formation carriers | Repeated precise components and recognizable three-dimensional subjects. |
| Supported fabric or printed paper | Period-appropriate flags and selected scenic textile details | Visible material contrast; banners need readable designs and durable attachments. |
| Paper/card | Historical deployment guide, command cards and scenario cards | Rules and history can be kept beside the object during play. |
| Texture paste, fine aggregate, adhesives, paint and finishes | Ground texture, permanent terrain assembly, protection and coloring | The workshop must specify compatible processes, sealed surfaces and finish samples. |

These are proposed allocations, not a final BOM. The design agent should choose real stock and quantify every installed and loose piece. Customers receive a finished landscape and finished figures. Repositioning a complete figure is play, not customer assembly.

Most small detailed components can be printed, while other materials provide most of the landscape's volume and surface character. Choose figure height and the printing process together before promising fine detail. Miniature paint schemes, material finishes and assembly registration are part of the workshop handoff, not work left for the customer. No motors, moving mechanisms or electronics are needed for the proposed play.

## Making reenactment work

The first rules prototype should use **formation groups** rather than require moving hundreds of individual soldiers. Several small, finished stands can represent one formation and be placed side by side, in depth, or in a defensive arrangement. The physical footprint must match the rule being represented. Clear handholds and stable bases matter more than miniature detail that prevents handling.

Six kinds of decision cover the requested experience:

1. **Formation:** change frontage, depth or defensive posture, spending time to reorganize.
2. **Facing and approach:** distinguish frontal pressure from an exposed flank or rear.
3. **Orders:** choose hold, advance, reposition, support or attack, with a limited ability to coordinate everything at once.
4. **Tempo:** trade rapid movement against readiness and cohesion.
5. **Reserves:** decide when and where to commit units whose later availability may be decisive.
6. **Morale and withdrawal:** permit a force to give ground, lose cohesion, rally or disengage rather than simply disappear on a hit.

This is a proposed original design vocabulary. Exact movement distances, dice, activation order, combat odds, victory thresholds and play length remain undecided. They must be developed together and tested. A short game should preserve meaningful decisions without claiming to simulate every historical variable.

Start with one documented deployment, a full-day scenario and several shorter scenarios. The proposals below change decisions or initial conditions; they do not assert proven alternative historical outcomes. Their premises come from the actions described in the historical timeline and battlefield account. [^30][^31]

| Scenario proposal | Decision being explored | What must remain honest |
|---|---|---|
| **Hougoumont: fix or bypass** | Decide how much to commit against the fortified farm and how much to retain for the main advance. | This should be a resource tradeoff, not a guaranteed shortcut. |
| **The infantry assault** | Change the grouping, approach and support of the main infantry attack. | Historical deployments and the game's alternative formations must be labeled separately. |
| **Horse, foot and guns** | Coordinate cavalry with infantry and artillery, or test the consequences of committing them separately. | Squares must have costs and weaknesses as well as defensive value; no automatic invulnerable formation. |
| **La Haye Sainte** | Contest the central strongpoint with different reinforcement and support priorities. | Model an objective with consequences for nearby approaches, not just a decorative building. |
| **The Prussian approach** | Change the arrival timing and route emphasis, and decide which French forces cover the eastern threat. | A counterfactual arrival schedule is not a claim about what Blücher actually ordered. |
| **The last reserve** | Commit elite reserves earlier, hold them longer, or use them in another sector. | The commitment must sacrifice flexibility elsewhere; it cannot be a scripted victory button. |

Players should be able to restore the display arrangement from a clear illustrated guide. Permanent oversized arrows, visible grids across every surface and a cluttered ring of game controls would compete with the living-room presentation. Use discreet removable aids and keep the default scene visually complete.

## The strongest alternatives

**Austerlitz** is the closest alternative if a tighter, more immediately legible tactical system becomes more important than Waterloo's breadth and recognition. Pratzen, the villages on the French right and supporting formations provide a strong linked problem. Jack Sigler's analysis directly discusses line and battalion-column arrangements. The game should still allow adaptation: Jacques Garnier cautions against the retrospective image of a perfectly foreseen plan. [^1][^2][^4]

**Sekigahara** would become the first choice if loyalty, recognizable clan identities and a tragic commander story become more important than combined-arms formation decisions. A tableau around Ōtani and Mt. Matsuo could be exceptional. The museum explicitly treats the warning-shot explanation for Kobayakawa's action as one theory; the game should not encode it as settled fact. [^6][^7]

**Marengo** would become the first choice if the desired emotional center is rescue, reversal and loss. Its French retreat, returning force and late counterattack offer a compact dramatic arc. The principal design problem is preventing replay from becoming a single scripted “wait for rescue” event. Formation, pursuit and command choices must still matter. [^8]

**Rivoli** would become the first choice if the object’s mountainous terrain is the leading purchase appeal. Multiple approaches can make concentration and timing visible. The design must preserve access routes rather than turn the plateau into an attractive but tactically arbitrary rock sculpture. [^9]

**Alesia** is the strongest later companion product for the workshop's material-and-construction identity. Its walls, earthworks, camps and double-facing defensive system have direct physical meaning. Archaeology supports the siegeworks; the later Gallo-Roman monumental town must not be imported into the 52 BCE scene. [^11][^12][^29]

## Historical and product evidence boundaries

Separate sourced history, reconstructed detail and invented game rules. Use a compact source note for each scenario. Do not present precise timing, troop counts or famous quotations as uncontested where accounts differ. For Waterloo, consult British, continental coalition and French material and keep the Prussian action visible. A later painting, existing game or famous scale model can be a research lead without becoming the authority for the whole reconstruction.

The first prototype needs tests for figure handling, readable formations, route width, stability, reset, rules comprehension and whether two different plausible strategies actually create different choices. A digital render can establish composition and geometry; it cannot establish enjoyable play, physical durability or customer demand.

Selection is complete at the concept level: **Waterloo is recommended for the broad-recognition, varied-force, formation-and-command brief.** The historical product remains to be designed. Its manufacture, play balance and commercial appeal are not yet validated.
