Atlas Expedition — The Lost Archipelago

Build one original, finished mixed-material tabletop exploration puzzle: a beautiful lost archipelago whose terrain and material clues invite players to explore, make rubbings, decode discoveries and plan routes. This is our second product after Waterloo Command. Make it a display-worthy miniature world with satisfying repeat play, not an assembly kit or an engraved puzzle board with scenery glued on.

This revised static Wish supersedes the mechanical direction of our paused Atlas Vault concept for this new product. The earlier design's useful starting ideas are supplied here, so accessing its private workspace is not a dependency: an irregular dark-blue wooden sea roughly 360 x 270 mm, four unequal terraced islands, an ivory plaster rubbing cove, an angular mirror cliff, tactile clay route markers, paper rubbing strips and a narrative based on discovering an unfamiliar shore language. Design fresh, fair clues and an open, ambient-light viewing arrangement for this static product.

Static scope
The permanently assembled landscape and all installed product components stay fixed during play. Players may pick up and reposition complete freestanding explorers, a boat, expedition markers, paper maps and rubbing sheets. Those are ordinary loose game pieces; they do not actuate anything. No hinges, sliding carriages, rotating dials, latches, springs, elastic launchers, motors, batteries, switches, wiring, ball tracks or powered effects. Do not invent moving mechanisms to make the scene feel interactive. The interest comes from observation, tactile discovery, spatial reasoning, route choice and a changing scenario.

The miniature world
Aim for a compact table/living-room object, approximately 360 x 270 mm and no more than 400 x 300 mm unless a specific usability need warrants a small change. Give the islands distinct height, outline, vegetation, architectural identity and negative space. Include memorable three-dimensional landmarks such as a ruined observatory, a sea gate or a cliff settlement; choose a coherent original civilization and story rather than a collection of generic monuments. Printed details and characters should read as intentional sculptural forms. Keep the finite component count and workshop assembly practical. Prefer two explorers, one small boat and a restrained set of generous discovery markers over armies of tiny decorative parts. The scene should be recognizable and desirable before anyone reads the rules.

A fair, replayable discovery
Make a coherent chain: locate a clue in the landscape, take a rubbing from an accessible relief, decode its symbols using another observable feature, then use that information to choose a destination or solve a route. A fixed mirror may make reversed signs readable in ordinary indoor ambient light. The mirror should be an open, accessible arrangement with enough viewing and paper-handling room, not the former shaded powered chamber. Specify the real reflective face, guarded edges, orientation and nominal viewing geometry. Distinguish geometric plausibility from human-tested optical legibility. Printed geometry or a composited reflected image must not pretend to be an observed mirror effect.

Provide one clear introductory expedition and three genuinely different follow-on scenarios using the same finished landscape and pieces. Change which information matters, the route constraints and the deductions; changing only a code or the story title is insufficient. Keep clues fair, accessible and logically solvable from the supplied materials. Include complete setup, turn/action rules if needed, success condition, reset, hints and separately presented solutions. Scenario cards or a solution booklet provide the confirmation/reveal. No mechanical unlocking is required. Perform deterministic logic/inventory checks where useful and a native rules walkthrough, without claiming human play testing. All required play pieces, rubbing paper and marking implements belong to the finished offering.

Materials with real jobs
Use wood/plywood for the base and layered terrain; 3D-printed custom architecture, figures, registration features and detail; cast plaster for accessible information-bearing relief; an appropriate supported clay medium for distinctive tactile/scenic elements; paper/card for maps, rubbings, scenarios and solutions; felt for protective contact surfaces; and a properly specified bought mirror where it serves the clue. Paint and compatible adhesives are finishing/assembly consumables. Select practical stock thicknesses and real material specifications. Do not add unused material families just to inflate coverage. Casting molds and fabrication jigs are internal workshop tools, not shipped play pieces. Reuse geometric motifs and material knowledge from the starting ideas, but validate the resulting current design and all current interfaces.
