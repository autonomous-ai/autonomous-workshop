Where four hours went

Two workshop fix runs on the same 24-part Anti-Sol board set, traced call by call. The spine is the Make session's own transcript, which timestamps every message: a tool call starts when the assistant issued it and ends when its result came back. 756 calls across the two runs, and between them the stretches where the model was generating. Nothing on the top two lanes is inferred.

Two questions the chart raised

Why frames land on both sides of the review

Because a frame being written and a frame being shown are different events, only the second opens the bracket — and because the review does not block. The critic is a background subagent: one Agent call opens it and returns immediately, so the session goes straight on to the assembly rounds and the verification sweep. It was busy for 87% of v12's bracket and 88% of v13's. This is the one thing in a correction that genuinely overlaps something else.

v12 rendered 98 frames and showed the critic 23. The hand-off is on disk: .tmp/build/images.txt, written at +1h35m, lists exactly 23 paths, and the last of them was written at +2h34m; the critic was dispatched three minutes later, at +2h37m. Round one's answer lands at +2h49m, and the session replies 24 seconds after that. The seven frames inside the bracket, at +3h29m to +3h35m, are not review inputs at all: they are saturn-dark-band-kept and -dropped, the same pair for the Anti-Sol army, saturn-cap-white and -beige, and venus-saturn-hero — the agent testing the twelve defects the critic had just filed. The critic never saw them. Its own record says rounds two and three are “disclosed re-reviews on the same frozen images”.

v13 does the opposite. It rendered twelve frames, all of them before the review, and copied eighteen — ten of its own, eight inherited — into .tmp/review/ under blind names frame-01.png … frame-18.png at +2h44m51s, dispatching the critic twelve seconds later. Two more, jupiter-sol-polar and jupiter-anti-polar, were rendered at +31m and +36m and deliberately held out of that set; they reached the critic at +3h00m, an hour into the review, as extra-A.png and extra-B.png. Nothing was re-rendered — a file already on disk was released later. The vertical rule inside v13's bracket is that hand-off.

And the first cut of this chart was wrong about it. It bounded the bracket with snap.glob("*.png"), which does not recurse, so it saw only the four frames at the top of snap/ and missed the 94 in snap/worlds/. In v12 that put the left edge on rank-ladder-anti.png at +1h23m and drew a 3h10m review where the Agent dispatch puts it at 1h55m.

Where the apparent silence went

It was never in the run. It was in the instrument. This page was built from cad/measure/**, which only exists for what make_round ran — 16% of v12 and 17% of v13. The Make session keeps a full timestamped transcript, and against that the accounting closes:

v12 is 75% sleep. It launched its build pipeline in the background and then polled it with 54 time.sleep(570) calls. That is not waste by itself — files were landing in 33 of those 54 sleeps, 200 of the 246 minutes — but it means the chart's old “model time” residue was mostly a background job the trace could not see. Two minutes of the whole run are unaccounted for.

The polling granularity does cost something, and it is small. The measures batch finished at +3h35m52s and the poll that reported it ended at +3h40m34s — 4m41s late. The component-round sweep finished at +3h52m25s and was reported at +3h59m48s — 7m22s late. Put beside v13's 67 seconds of timeout overshoot, about a quarter of an hour across both runs is spent asleep on work that had already finished: roughly 2%. 21 of the 54 sleeps, 46 minutes, passed with no file written anywhere in the workspace — the upper bound on sleeps that watched nothing, since compute that writes nothing still sits inside it.

v13 ran its heavy work in the foreground, and five calls blew the 600-second tool timeout — two make_round --require-component-passes, two verify_project --strict-fit and one world_views.py jupiter. The wording matters: “did not complete within its 600s timeout and was moved to the background.” The command finishes — verify_project's own report records 793 s for a call the agent gave up on at 601 s — and the four whose task files survive were picked up 5 to 23 seconds after they finished. The ceiling costs 67 seconds of overshoot, not 50 minutes. Those 50 minutes are the calls' duration, which was real work.

Nor does it explain the repeats, which is what an earlier version of this page said. make_round ran twice because round r0003 came back visual ERROR — Visual render failed or source changed; r0004 then reached the normal PENDING. verify_project ran four times and all four passed (680 s, 547 s, 549 s, 793 s — 42m40s), each a fresh sweep after the agent regenerated geometry. That cost belongs to re-verifying the whole product after every touch, not to the tool ceiling.

v13's one real gap is 46.9 minutes, from +53m to +1h41m, and it is the only stretch in either run where nothing whatever happens: no tool call, no model message, and not one file written anywhere under the run root. A session teardown killed the run and workshop resume restarted it. Everything else that looked empty was a call in flight.

Why one frame costs a minute

Rendering is the single largest cost in both runs — roughly two hours of v12's 5h30m (28m of make_round renders measured from their own logs, plus a 95-minute window in which 91 frames landed) and 59m of v13's 4h15m, where the calls are in the foreground and can be read off directly. None of it is graphics hardware: render_review rasterises in software, NumPy and Pillow, no GL context. The reason it is slow is not the picture.

Two things multiply. First, these frames deliberately tessellate ten times finer than the default: world_views.py passes an angular tolerance of 0.03 rad instead of build123d's 0.1, because at 0.1 a polar cap reads as about twenty visible facet rings. The comment records the measurement — the same globe is 138,911 triangles at the default and 1,357,677 at 0.03. Second, render_review.render loops over triangles in Python, allocating several small NumPy arrays inside the loop.

Rendering a synthetic mesh through the repo's own renderer puts a number on it:

triangles900 px per triangle
14,4000.76 s52.6 µs
78,4003.91 s49.9 µs
360,00018.07 s50.2 µs
1,537,60074.71 s48.6 µs

~50 µs per triangle, flat. 1.36 M triangles × 50 µs = 68 seconds, which is exactly the spacing between consecutive world frames in v12. The eight-globe rank ladder is eight such pieces — 10.9 M triangles, 545 s predicted — and v13's world_views.py ladder call took 504 s.

The image size does not matter. The same 360,000-triangle mesh takes 19.1 s at 150 px and 17.5 s at 1800 px. There is no pixel bottleneck; the whole cost is per-triangle Python and NumPy call overhead. A profile of that render says where it sits:

of 28.2 ssecondswhat
np.cross9.85 one call per triangle for the face normal — 35% of the render, on 3-vectors
the loop body itself10.1indexing, bounds, the block loop
np.ogrid3.48a fresh pixel grid per triangle row-block
_shade3.25one shading call per triangle

Every one of those was a per-face call that could be one array call over all faces, and on 2026-09-21 they became one. render_review now computes face normals and shading in single array calls and rasterises faces in batches, with each pixel's candidates still applied in draw order so the depth test keeps its hysteresis. On the same synthetic mesh a 1.44 M-triangle frame at 900 px falls from 83 s to 2.2 s — 58 µs to 1.6 µs per triangle, 37× — with pixel-identical output. These runs predate the change: their renders are what the per-face pass cost.

Ten longest spans

How this was measured

Tool calls come from the Make session's transcript, which timestamps every message. A call starts at the assistant message that issued it and ends at the result that came back, paired by tool_use_id so a message issuing several at once is counted once each. 756 calls. Only timestamps, tool names and a path-stripped command label are carried onto this page.

Model generating is the interval between a result arriving and the next assistant message, where no call was open. It is a reading, not a residue.

Tool spans — the lanes under the rule — come from the run's own logs. Each begins with the command and carries a line like (685.9s, exit 0); the file's mtime is when it finished, so the start is the end minus the duration. 716 spans, all of them inside a tool call on the lane above.

Carried spans are the ones a correction imports from its source archive. They all share one mtime — the instant the import copied them — so they are drawn as a single block at that instant, not as work this run performed. Their durations are the source run's.

Overshoot — how long a finished background job waited to be noticed — is the mtime of its tasks/<id>.output against the end of the call that read it. v13's four surviving task files give 18 s, 5 s, 23 s and 21 s. v12's were cleaned up, so its overshoot is measured instead from the last file each job wrote against the poll that first reported it done.

The blind review is a background subagent. The transcript shows exactly one Agent call opening it — +2h37m in v12, +2h45m in v13 — returning in about a second, then two and four SendMessage calls back to it, then the verdict file. The bracket runs from that dispatch to the verdict; the review's own duration is recorded nowhere, and the session is doing other work for most of the window. The frames it read are on disk either way: v12 listed its 23 in .tmp/build/images.txt, v13 copied its 18 into .tmp/review/ under blind names.

Frame renders are not spans. The agent runs them directly, so nothing logs a duration. They appear as diamonds at the moment each file was written; the gap back to the previous frame is an upper bound, since model time sits in it too. In v12 all 98 were rendered; in v13, 86 were carried from the source archive and 12 re-rendered. Filled diamonds are the frames the critic was shown — 23 of v12's 98, 18 of v13's. The hollow ones are the agent's own working renders.

A gap is not a blank. Every file in the workspace carries an mtime, so a stretch with no tool span still says what was written in it and when. The grey blocks inside each hatched band are those writes, grouped into contiguous runs and named after whatever wrote the most files in the run. They are positions, not durations.

Not measured: tokens. Both archives carry TOKENS.json: unavailable, so this page can say how long the model spent generating but not how much it generated. Earlier versions treated the unexplained remainder as model time; the transcript shows most of it was a background job instead.
