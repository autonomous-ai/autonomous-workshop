# Antisol Ouranos

Jungle Chess played with the solar system, in two armies of eight worlds.

A planet's size on the board is the fifth root of its real measured diameter,
so rank is something you can see rather than something you have to learn:
Mercury is the smallest world in play and Jupiter the largest. The traditional
rules are followed exactly as written. Matter faces antimatter -- one army's
worlds lean their poles one way and the other army's lean the other, by each
planet's own true axial tilt, and the bases say the same thing again in white
against black.

Four of the eight worlds wear a real map: Earth its own coastlines, Mars its
own dark continents, Mercury its own smooth plains with the Caloris basin in
them, and Venus -- alone in the set -- a surface no eye has ever seen. The
other four wear their weather.

Uranus is the world this edition corrects, and the correction is two things at
once. The first is that its one marking was in the wrong place and the wrong
shape. It used to wear a single white band around its equator -- and Uranus
lies on its side, ninety-eight degrees over, so that equatorial band stood
UPRIGHT on the face you were looking at. A bold white upright stripe on a
bright cyan ball is a tennis ball, and in the old box photograph the two
Uranus pieces were among the most toy-like objects on the board.

Uranus's own photograph shows nothing of the sort: a pale, almost uniform
sphere carrying one very faint lighter region, soft-edged, off-centre, with no
stripe anywhere on it. And there is a reason for that beyond taste. A band is
the wrong feature for this planet. Because it lies on its side, Uranus points a
pole at the Sun for forty years at a time, and what its atmosphere actually
shows is a **polar hood** -- a brighter region around whichever pole is facing
out. The same tilt that stood the old band upright turns a hood face-on. So the
band is gone and there is a broad soft hood at each pole instead, with its
boundary at sixty degrees of latitude, in the palest warm tone the box already
carries rather than in white.

**Both poles, and that was measured rather than chosen.** The two armies lean
opposite ways, so whatever one turns toward you the other turns away. A hood on
the north pole alone sits a hundred and twenty-five degrees behind the limb on
every piece of the Anti-Sol army at both angles the set is photographed from --
a marking one whole army would never show. Two hoods is also what the planet
has, since the bright region follows whichever pole faces the Sun and over one
orbit that is both.

**And Uranus now has a ring.** That is the owner's decision rather than
anything the photograph shows, and it is written down as exactly that: the
reference carries no ring, and every other correction in this set forbids
inventing what the reference does not show. It is defensible on its own terms,
because Uranus really does have thirteen narrow rings -- they are simply too
faint for the picture to carry.

What makes it worth having is the orientation, and the orientation is free. A
ring lies in its planet's equatorial plane. Uranus's equatorial plane stands
nearly vertical, so its ring is **an upright hoop standing over the globe, not
a brim beside it** -- which is what Uranus genuinely looks like, and what stops
the piece reading as a second Saturn. Saturn's ring is a near-horizontal plate
and the two cannot be confused in silhouette even before size enters it: Ø30.00
against Ø24.02, with Saturn still the widest world in the box. The hoop
projects one millimetre per side, a little over half Saturn's two, because the
instruction was that it be small.

Small is the hard part. Everything below the base is cut away, and a small hoop
meets that cut on the steep part of its own curve: the rim leaves the base at
48.6 degrees from vertical, past the 45 degrees a printer holds unaided, and
does not clear it until half a millimetre higher. Growing the ring until the
cut lands somewhere shallower takes it straight back to Saturn's own size. So
the junction carries a small wedge instead, at the same 47-degree slope the
box already trusts under Saturn's ring. The steepest downward-facing angle
anywhere on the finished hoop is 44.3 degrees and it passes.

**Two things about this piece are stated rather than hidden.**

The globe's colour is wrong and cannot be made right here. The reference is a
pale desaturated ice blue; the filament is `cyan`, a saturated neon. There is
no pale blue in the thirteen PLA colours this box is printed from -- the only
other blue is a dark navy -- and the one pale-ish blue the supplier lists at
all is a different material family, and a mid slate rather than an ice blue in
any case. No choice of marking makes a neon globe paler, and none was
attempted. If the set should acquire a pale blue, that is a recommendation for
a future edition: `cyan` is shared with the Anti-Sol flames and corona cells,
so changing it reaches well beyond two pieces.

And the hood's edge is hard where the reference's is soft. Every marking in
this box is a flush colour inlay: the colour changes at a line, and the
narrowest line the nozzle can lay is four tenths of a millimetre, which is two
degrees of arc on this globe. A brightening that fades out cannot be printed.
Lowering the contrast is the substitute rather than the cure, and the tone was
measured against the globe rather than picked.

Nothing else in the set changed: the other fourteen worlds, the board, the
belt, the coronas, the stars and the trays come out of this run with exactly
the geometry they had, and that is measured file by file rather than asserted.
This is the first edition in this series whose two corrected pieces were
EXPECTED to change shape. Every earlier one moved a colour boundary, which
partitions a globe without moving its surface, so the corrected pieces came out
byte-for-byte identical and that was the right answer. A ring is added
material. Both Uranus pieces changed; the other twenty-two printed geometries
did not.

The corrections before this one stand untouched. Neptune's clouds were cut
into short wisps, Saturn's bands came down in tone rather than up, Jupiter's
belts were put back at their own unequal latitudes, and Earth, Mars, Mercury
and Venus each wear a real map; none of that was reopened here.

**Saturn's ring is untouched.** It already worked, it is what the whole set's
size ladder was solved against, and not a number in it moved. This box now has
two worlds with rings, and the orientation is what tells them apart: Saturn's
is a plate lying almost flat, Uranus's is a hoop standing on edge, because each
ring lies in its own planet's equatorial plane and those two planets lean
twenty-seven degrees and ninety-eight. They are not the same kind of part,
though, and that is worth saying: Saturn's ring is a separate white piece and
Uranus's is the globe's own colour, so one reads as something added and the
other as something the planet has.

A cocoa-brown asteroid belt crosses the middle of the field, a star stands at
each end with two flames bursting off the board edge, and the three cells
around each star are sunken corona wells where a captured world drops out of
the light and its rank counts for nothing. You win by walking one of your
worlds onto the rival star.

## What arrives

Forty-two printed parts: sixteen worlds, a board in four panels, twelve
asteroid-belt tiles, two stars, six corona cells and two storage trays. Nothing
is glued, screwed or assembled; the tiles and stars drop into their own pockets
and the panels butt together.

## Where the eight surfaces come from

**All sixteen world pieces have now been corrected, one pair at a time, across
eight runs.** A reader picking the set up should be able to learn in one
paragraph where each world's surface comes from, without reading eight revision
histories, so here is all eight worlds in one line each.

- **Mercury** -- outline. Seven smooth albedo plains traced as rings, plus the
  Caloris basin as a bright floor inside a raised rim.
- **Venus** -- outline. Seven radar highland provinces led by Aphrodite Terra,
  and three lowland plains, from the Magellan mosaic.
- **Earth** -- outline. Coastlines, dry interiors and a northern ice cap.
- **Mars** -- outline plus cap. The classical albedo map, and two polar caps
  whose rims are broken by lobes so they read as ice rather than as lids.
- **Jupiter** -- band, blob and outline. Six belts at Jupiter's own unequal
  latitudes, five, seven, ten, thirteen, seven and six degrees wide; five
  bright cream zones between and beyond them; and the Great Red Spot as one
  oval, thirteen degrees of arc by nine, with a darker collar and the widest
  belt bending north around it. The two widest belts carry a slight wave rather
  than running as exact circles.
- **Saturn** -- band, cap and **ring**. Five bands at nine, fifteen, eight,
  sixteen and twelve degrees wide, unevenly spaced and not symmetric about the
  equator; four of them a mid amber and only the widest still brown, because
  this is the world in the box whose reference is softer than the build was;
  and a bright cap above fifty-eight degrees north. The ring is geometry rather
  than a marking: a flat plate in Saturn's equatorial plane, so it lies almost
  level.
- **Uranus** -- **cap** at each pole, and **ring**. A broad soft polar hood
  above and below sixty degrees of latitude, in the palest warm tone the box
  carries; both poles, because the two armies lean opposite ways and one hood
  would be invisible on half the pieces. The ring is geometry rather than a
  marking: a hoop in Uranus's equatorial plane, which on a planet lying on its
  side means it stands upright over the globe. It prints in the globe's own
  colour, so it reads as relief rather than as a line drawn on the planet.
- **Neptune** -- outline. Eight short white cloud wisps, forty-six to ninety
  degrees of longitude long, no two the same, at eight unevenly spaced
  latitudes from fifty-two degrees south to forty-seven north, each tilted and
  bowed a few degrees off its own line of latitude and tapering to a rounded
  end; and the Great Dark Spot as one oval twice as wide as it is tall, capped
  by the small bright companion cloud the reference shows. Not one streak
  circles the planet.

No world is bare, and none of the eight is drawn as a union of circles.

**The useful division is not which were corrected but where each surface comes
from.** Four of these worlds have a solid surface that has really been mapped
-- Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars -- so their markings are traced outlines, and
on Earth and Mars they are silhouettes you can check against a map you already
know. The other four are atmosphere. Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune have
no surface to trace; what they show is weather, so their markings are bands,
caps, storms and cloud arcs. That is why the first four are outlines and the
last four are not, and it is the one thing worth carrying away from this
section.

All eight were corrected, and the four atmosphere worlds were corrected for
different reasons than the four mapped ones. The mapped four had the wrong KIND
of marking: each was a union of round patches where it should have been a
traced outline. The atmosphere four each had the right kind of marking drawn
too regularly or in the wrong place -- Jupiter's belts evenly spaced where the
real ones are not, Saturn's four bands equal in width and too dark, Neptune's
three closed bands where the real clouds are short arcs, and Uranus's one
equatorial band where the planet's own feature is a polar hood. Regularity is
the single thing that tells a reader they are holding a manufactured object,
and that is what those four runs were about.

## Before you print

Read `product.json` for the full list of limitations. Nothing here has been
printed, handled or played. Every fit, wall thickness and overhang margin is a
measurement on the exact CAD solids and a prediction for a 0.4 mm nozzle at
0.2 mm layers.

**The verification finished, and every check that could run did.** The pipeline
ran for 723 seconds over 11 steps: seven ran and passed, three are not
applicable and are recorded as not run rather than as passed, and none was left
unverified. The geometry inspection completed all 51 of its checks and was not
interrupted — that includes validating every one of the 236 pieces in the
assembled set for sound topology and positive volume, and checking the whole
assembly for interference, which is the only way to answer whether any two
parts occupy the same space. Nothing does. Of the 723 seconds, 280 went on
regenerating every part from source and 426 on the single inspection batch.

Separately, in their own rounds, all 24 printed parts cleared the 0.80 mm wall
gate and the 45-degree overhang gate at a 0.4 mm nozzle — 24 of 24 on each,
with no region anywhere needing support and no bridges. The new ring is covered
by that: its steepest downward-facing surface measures 44.3 degrees against the
45-degree limit.

The three checks that were not run are recorded as not run rather than as
passed: the mounting and power checks, because there is no bought part and no
powered system in this set to check, and the motion check, which is switched
off for this build — so **motion is unverified**, not passed. Nothing in the
set moves against anything else in any case.

**None of that makes this print-ready, and it is not claimed to be.** Every
number above is geometry. No part of this set has been printed, held, or
played with.

Saturn takes four filaments -- `yellow`, `sunflower_yellow`, `cocoa_brown`
and `white` -- where it took three, and it is the only world in the box whose
correction lowered its contrast rather than raising it. What the new band tone
is worth is measured rather than assumed:
`cad/measure/saturn-tone-separation.md` renders one Saturn piece twice at one
camera with only the bands repainted between the two, so the pixels that move
are the bands and nothing else moves at all. The amber separates **9.4** of 255
greyscale levels from the gold globe, which is the smallest separation this
project has ever committed to and smaller than the 21.8 it once rejected on
Mercury as invisible. That number is given rather than buried, because it is
the point: this world is supposed to be quiet. It reads because the bands are
enormous -- about 102,000 pixels of a 900-pixel frame, where Mercury's patches
were a few millimetres across -- and because an amber against a gold carries a
change of hue that a greyscale number does not count. The fallback tone the
brief allowed, `beige`, measured 10.1, seven tenths of a level better, and was
not taken.

One thing that costs, stated plainly. Saturn's band amber is the same
`sunflower_yellow` that is Venus's entire globe, so from this run the two
pieces are built out of the same spools.
`cad/measure/venus-saturn-separation.md` puts them side by side at the
product's own frame afterwards and answers on three counts separately: **size
and silhouette tell them apart easily** -- Saturn's globe is 9.47 mm larger, it
stands a third taller in the picture, and it wears a ring Venus has nothing
like -- and **the surface does not**. The surface is the weakest of the three
cues and this correction made it weaker. What still separates them there is
which way round the colours sit: Venus is a flat amber ball with one pale
province on it, Saturn is a lighter gold ball wearing the amber as soft stripes
with a white cap over its north.

Uranus still takes two filaments -- `cyan` for the globe and `beige` for the
hoods -- because the ring prints in the globe's own cyan. That was measured
rather than assumed, and it is the most interesting thing this correction
learned. The ring began white, which is what this box's other ring wears. An
independent reader, shown only the pictures and not told what the set was,
named both Uranus pieces as tennis balls and said exactly why: a bold white
curve on a saturated ball next to a pale panel. Grey was tried and read as a
*more* convincing seam, because a tennis seam is a dark curve on a bright ball.
Neither tone predicted the reading; what did was the ring being a different
material from the ball. In the globe's own colour the hoop stops being a line
drawn on the planet and becomes relief -- you read it by its silhouette and its
own shadow -- and because its brightness against the ball barely changed, it is
no harder to see across a table. The same reader's third look reported the
tennis ball gone and the ring still plainly a ring.
What the hood tone is worth was measured the same way Saturn's bands were:
`cad/measure/uranus-tone-separation.md` renders one Uranus piece twice at one
camera with only the hoods repainted, against every one of the thirteen
filaments. `beige` separates 29.0 of 255 greyscale levels from the cyan globe
and the `white` it replaces separates 35.2, so the tone change on its own is
about a fifth quieter -- real, but not what stops the piece reading as a tennis
ball. What does that is the shape: a closed belt across the face became a broad
region at each pole with no closed boundary anywhere on the globe. A quieter
stripe would still have been a stripe.

Jupiter takes four filaments -- `orange`, `cocoa_brown`, `beige` and `red` --
for the reason measured in `cad/measure/jupiter-tone-separation.md`. Venus takes
three -- `sunflower_yellow`, `beige` and `cocoa_brown` -- where it used to take
two, and `beige` on that amber separates 20.2 greyscale levels, which was the
thinnest in the set until this run. Mercury is the other world that takes three
-- grey, cocoa brown and white -- for the reason measured in
`cad/measure/mercury-tone-separation.md`.

**What Saturn's correction could not do, and Neptune's could not either.** A
flush colour inlay has a hard edge and there is no way to make it soft. The reference's bands fade into one another;
these cannot, and every boundary on this globe is a colour change at a line.
Lowering the contrast is the substitute for softening the edge, not a cure for
it. Nothing else the planet shows was added: no hexagonal polar vortex, no
storms, no spokes in the ring, no ring divisions and no shadow of the ring on
the globe. Each was considered and each was rejected on arithmetic --
`cad/measure/saturn-atlas-resolution.md` carries it, the hexagon included,
whose visible edge is a quarter of one nozzle width.

**What Neptune's correction could not do.** Three things, all stated rather
than worked around, and all three found by an independent reader who was shown
the renders and told nothing else.

The white marks read as paint rather than as weather. That is the 1.5 mm width
decision above meeting a flush inlay's hard edge, and neither is fixable on
this globe: the width was chosen deliberately for legibility at the set's own
scale, and every colour boundary anywhere in this box is a change of filament
at a line.

The companion cloud does not yet read as the spot's own. Every number it was
asked for is met -- white, on the spot's equator-facing side, half its length,
six tenths of a millimetre clear of it, which is a nozzle and a half -- and it
was redrawn three times, ending as a crescent parallel to the spot's rim. A
reader shown it cold still called it a stray fragment. The obstacle looks like
contrast rather than placement: `dark_gray` on `blue` separates only 42 of 255
greyscale levels, so the spot reads as a grey smudge, and a bright mark beside
a smudge has nothing to belong to. The correction fixes the spot's filament, so
this run could not test the alternative; `cad/snap/SIGNATURE-REVIEW.json`
carries the whole finding and what would change it.

And both Neptunes have a bare hemisphere. Every streak and the spot are placed
where the set's two photographed frames can see them, which is what the
correction asks for, so the opposite face carries the end of one streak and
bare blue globe. The eight streaks and the spot cover 211 of 360 degrees of
longitude, close to the most that constraint allows.
`cad/snap/worlds/neptune-sol-opposite.png` is that picture -- and it is also
the proof of the correction, because a closed band would have been there in
full. What the piece does NOT carry is a second dark spot, a polar brightening,
a ring arc, or the faint darker centre inside the spot that the reference
shows; the last of those would have needed a fourth filament on a globe held
to three.

**What Uranus's correction could not do.** Three things, all stated rather than
worked around.

The globe's colour stays wrong. `cyan` #00FFFF is a saturated neon and
`cad/ref/uranus-sol.png` is a pale desaturated ice blue -- its ball means
161, 200, 206 against the filament's 0, 255, 255, measured in
`cad/measure/uranus-reference.md`. The thirteen PLA colours this box prints
from are the supplier's whole PLA range and none of them is a pale blue; the
only other blue is a dark navy, further away than the cyan is. The one
pale-ish blue the supplier lists at all belongs to a different material family
and is a mid slate in any case. No marking makes a neon globe paler and none
was attempted.

The hood's edge is hard where the reference's is soft, for the reason every
marking in this box has a hard edge: it is a change of filament at a line, and
the narrowest line the nozzle lays is two degrees of arc on this globe.

And the ring is not in the reference at all. It is an owner instruction,
recorded as one here, in `product.json`, in the design contract and in
`cad/measure/uranus-ring.md`. Nothing else about the real ring system is drawn:
thirteen narrow rings at a fifth of a millimetre per degree of arc would be
grit, so what the piece carries is one solid hoop. No moons, no storms, no
banding, no shepherd gaps and no named ringlets.

One thing two hoods cost, stated plainly. A hood at each pole makes this
world's surface symmetric, so at the two angles the set is photographed from
the two armies show the same face. Saturn's single northern cap is an ownership
cue in its own right and Uranus gives that up. It gives it up because a
northern hood alone is not a cue on this world -- it is a marking one army
never shows. What still tells the two apart here is what tells them apart on
the six worlds with no cap at all: the base, white against black, and the
numeral in the other tone -- plus one thing those six do not have, which is
that the hoop leans the other way.
