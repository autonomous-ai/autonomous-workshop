# The independent blind review of this revision

`snap/SIGNATURE-REVIEW.json` is the sealed record and its fields are
length-bounded. This is the same account at full length, so that nothing had to
be cut to fit a field. It replaces the file of the same name in the source
archive, which described the review of the Neptune BANDS revision; that account
is superseded here and this run used none of it as evidence.

## Who reviewed it, and what was different about the frame set

ONE independent native critic, THREE rounds, shown only the twenty canonical
renders of this build and told nothing else. It was instructed to open no
source, no spec, no report, no JSON and no directory listing, to run no shell
command, and not to guess from the folder names in the paths it was given. It
confirmed at the end of every round that it had not, and named the standing
instruction it overrode to comply:

> "I opened only image files from the twenty you named ... I opened no other
> file of any kind, listed no directory, and **ran no shell command**, again
> overriding the standing instruction to prefer Bash."

**There were THREE rounds and ZERO repairs.** No geometry changed at any point.
The images the critic read blind in round 1 are the images sealed in
`SIGNATURE-REVIEW.json`. Rounds 2 and 3 were disclosed comparison rounds against
the same frozen pictures, and each is marked as a disclosed re-review rather
than a naive read. Nothing in this document claims a second unprimed reading.

**The frame set was deliberately rebalanced, and that is the one methodological
change this run makes.** The first attempt at this same brief built the object
correctly and could not seal, because its critic was shown sixteen frames of
which twelve were detail views of one world, and not one of them let the eight
globes be compared at a single scale. This set's whole ownership claim is that a
planet's size on the board is the fifth root of its real diameter, so rank is
something you can SEE; a critic asked whether that arrives, and handed almost
nothing but close-ups of one piece, cannot say yes. So this run added
`snap/rank-ladder-sol.png` and `snap/rank-ladder-anti.png` -- all eight worlds of
one army at one orthographic camera and one framing box, in rank order -- and
put them in the round-one set. Of the twenty frames the critic was given, ten
show the whole object or two worlds together and ten show the corrected world
alone. The ladder frames photograph the ladder and do not touch it:
`LADDER_CONSTANT`, `LADDER_EXPONENT` and every `globe_d` are read and never
written.

## Round one, unprimed

The critic was given twenty images, eight open questions and five specific ones,
and told nothing about the object. Cold, it identified a two-player abstract
board game played with the planets, counted eight pieces a side, read the
lift-and-place action, and **named all eight worlds from cues it recorded at the
time** -- Jupiter from its bands and red spot, Saturn from its ring, Uranus from
"a featureless pale cyan sphere with a narrow ring standing nearly vertical ...
Uranus is the tipped-over planet with a perpendicular ring plane", Neptune from
its dark spot, Earth from "the shape of Africa at roughly (600,290)", then
Venus, Mars and Mercury. It discovered the parity inversion unaided. It listed
twenty-nine defects. And it answered this revision's two decisive questions
before it knew either of them was decisive.

## The decisive negative requirement, answered blind

The requirement is that NOTHING is drawn on either Uranus globe. A negative is
the kind of requirement a render can miss -- a marking that silently fails to
cut leaves a piece that still measures as sound, complete and correctly sized,
and `parts/world.py` carries that exact scar from this very world. So it was put
to the critic cold, as its own question, with the four polar frames named as the
place a polar marking would be most conspicuous:

> "**NO. Nothing at all. Confidence: very high -- as high as I can be from
> images.** The cyan ball is a **completely uniform, unmarked, flat cyan
> sphere**. The only variation anywhere on its surface is a smooth diffuse
> lighting gradient ... and the shadow cast onto it by the hoop. There is no
> band, no patch, no cap, no panel, no spot, no stripe, no seam line, no pole
> marking, and no change of colour of any kind."

It logged all eight close frames one at a time with the ball's pixel bounds in
each, and on the four polar frames:

> "**the pole faces me directly and is bare** ... No cap, no ring around the
> pole, no spot, no dot, no change of shade beyond the lighting falloff."
>
> "all **four poles of both copies** are presented face-on and centred, and
> **all four are blank**. If there were any polar treatment at all it would be
> unmissable in those frames. There is none."

And it calibrated its own instrument, unprompted, which is what makes the
negative evidence worth having:

> "For calibration, the other pieces in this set clearly *can* carry surface
> markings -- Earth has continents and white caps, Jupiter has a red spot,
> Neptune has a dark oval, Mars has patches. So this is not a limitation of the
> rendering or the process."

`measure/uranus-bare.md` asks the same question of the solids, three ways, and
agrees.

## The beach ball, which is the point of this revision

The Wish required that the question be put to the blind review as its own
question, unprimed, at board distance and close up; that the answer be reported
in the words it comes back in; that the ring's filament NOT be changed back
whatever the answer; and that nothing be added, tinted or softened to
compensate. All four held.

Asked cold in round 1, before the critic knew what the object was or that the
question mattered, **the answer is yes at board distance**:

> "**at board distance, beach ball, both cyan pieces** (plus tennis ball for the
> blue banded piece)."

Its mechanism, in its own words:

> "a saturated, completely flat, glossy single-colour sphere with **one bright
> white stripe running over it**. That is the exact visual grammar of an
> inflatable beach ball: solid bright panel colour, hard-edged contrasting band,
> no surface detail whatsoever. At this distance the standing hoop is too thin to
> read as a separate object, so the eye collapses it into a painted stripe.
> Nothing in the picture says 'planet'; everything says 'toy ball'."

Close up the reading dissolves, and it splits by viewing angle:

> "Close up, **not a beach ball** -- a ball in a standing hoop from three-quarter
> views, and a seamed moulded ball from straight-on views."

In round 2 it was told what it had not known: that the edition being reversed
went to `cyan` because an earlier reviewer had called both pieces tennis balls
and named a mechanism with TWO halves -- "a bold white curved line arcing down
one side of a saturated-colour ball, and a large pale panel on the opposite
side" -- and that this revision removed one of those halves, the pale panel
being the polar hood. It did not move, and it sharpened the finding against the
object rather than for it:

> "My mechanism has **three** clauses: flat single colour, one high-contrast
> band, and **'no surface detail whatsoever.'** That third clause is the
> problem. It is not a half of the earlier reviewer's mechanism that survived
> the revision -- **it is a clause that the revision created.** The hood was the
> only thing on that globe that was not flat colour. Removing it did remove half
> of the earlier reviewer's mechanism; it also removed the only counter-evidence
> against mine."
>
> "**The revision swapped one route to the toy-ball reading for another.** The
> earlier reviewer got there via *ball with a stripe and a panel*. I got there
> via *ball with a stripe and nothing else at all*. Both routes end in the same
> place, and the second one is the one the revision built."

It also made a point about the measurement that is worth keeping, because it
cuts against the easy reading of that number:

> "38.7-39.3 luma levels of separation is a **clean, crisp, high-contrast
> band**. That number was measured to prove the ring is legible, and it does
> prove it. But crisp high-contrast is also precisely what makes a band read as
> a moulded panel line rather than as a physical hoop. **M7 quantifies the
> ring's legibility and the beach-ball mechanism with the same number.**"

One thing did move in the object's favour once it knew the materials: the
close-up reading it had given charitably turns out to be simply correct.

> "Knowing now that the ring is white filament and the globe is cyan -- genuinely
> two materials, not paint -- the three-quarter close-up reading is *correct*,
> not merely charitable. The hoop really is a separate object and the eye really
> does resolve it at that distance. The failure is confined to board distance,
> where the hoop subtends too few pixels to resolve."

### The recommendation, for the owner to decide

Recorded as a recommendation and acted on in no way. **The ring is not changed
back, no marking was added, nothing was tinted and nothing was softened.**

> "**I would not touch the ring.** M7's separation is doing real work ...
> Reducing contrast solves the wrong problem and risks losing the ring
> entirely."
>
> "**The lever is the globe, not the ring.** ... So the question to put to the
> owner is narrower than 'hood or no hood': *Was the objection to having a polar
> marking, or to those two pale beige discs specifically?* If it was the pale
> discs ... then there is an option that honours the objection completely and
> still fixes the reading: **the same hood geometry at the same 60 degree
> boundary, rendered in a darker tone of the globe's own cyan rather than in
> beige.** No pale disc anywhere. No second colour introduced ... A darker polar
> region is also the correct direction physically -- limb and pole darkening is
> what an ice giant actually does."
>
> "If the objection was to having *any* polar marking, then I would say:
> **accept the reading and do nothing.** It is board-distance only; it resolves
> correctly in the hand; and 'looks a bit like a toy ball' is a soft failure in
> an object that is a toy. That is a defensible place to land and I would not
> argue with an owner who chose it."

And the argument it says it would actually lead with, which is not the beach
ball at all:

> "This globe is now **the only bare one in a set of sixteen where the other
> fifteen are painted** ... that asymmetry is the stronger argument for putting
> *something* back."

The measured separation that belongs beside all of this is in
`measure/uranus-ring-tone.md`: **`white` against `cyan` #00FFFF separates 38.7
to 39.3 of 255 greyscale levels** across the three canonical camera angles,
measured by building one piece once and rendering it twice with only the ring
repainted.

## The two ringed worlds, asked as the Wish required

The Wish asked that the review be asked how many ringed worlds it sees and
whether it can say which is which without being told, exactly as the Uranus run
did, so the two answers can be compared. Asked cold:

> "**Four pieces**, being **two subjects, one in each army**."
>
> "**Can I tell the two subjects apart without being told? Yes, trivially, and I
> did before reading anything.**"

Its cues, in the order it ranked them: colour ("This alone settles it at any
distance"), then ring attitude ("Horizontal brim vs upright band. This is the
*meaningful* difference and it is visible even in the tiny board-distance
renders"), then ring width, then size. And:

> "**Would I actually manage it across a table? Yes, easily** -- colour does it
> before I've even focused, and ring attitude confirms it. **This is the one
> piece of the design that works unambiguously at play distance.**"

In round 3, asked to choose the one visible thing that stops the set being
generic, it chose the rings over the ladder: "Seal the rings as the visible
signature" -- the ladder being "the better *idea* and the weaker *sighting*".

That is a stronger answer than the Uranus run's, and it is worth being precise
about why the comparison is not quite like for like: the colour cue it ranks
first is the globes' colours, cyan against cream, not the rings'. Both rings now
print `white`, so the RING's colour separates the two worlds by nothing at all.
`measure/uranus-saturn-separation.md` puts that question to the solids.

## Where the critic corrected the Manager, and the Manager was wrong

In round 2 the Manager put eleven requirements to the critic one at a time. One
of them was written wrongly, and the critic caught it.

The Manager's wording described Uranus's ring as "an upright hoop standing over
the ball **pole-over-pole**". That describes a meridian ring, which is a
different and wrong object. The critic rejected it, and its reasoning is the
reason the correction is in the source rather than only in this document:

> "in images 17-20 the hoop resolves into a **complete circle concentric with
> the ball**. To see a circle you must be looking along the ring's axis. Those
> frames are the polar views, so the ring's axis *is* the polar axis -- which
> makes this an **equatorial ring on a globe whose poles point sideways**, not a
> meridian ring passing over the poles."
>
> "I flag this because it is the whole point of this particular world ...
> **The object is right; the sentence isn't.**"

It is right. `measure/world_separation.py`'s ring-stance wording was corrected
and the three side-by-side reports regenerated. No geometry, no STEP and no
image changed; a sentence about them did. That correction was made after the
Manager's own visual rounds were recorded, and is disclosed here for that
reason.

Then, in round 3, the critic corroborated the corrected statement from pixel
coordinates it had written down in round 1, **before any angle had been quoted
to it**:

> "My round-1 message recorded the hoop's endpoints as *'~490,140 down to
> ~420,640'* ... That is 70 px of run over 500 px of rise: **atan(70/500) = 8.0
> degrees from vertical**, equal and opposite on the two copies. Against your
> stated 7.77 degrees ... I did not know the figure when I recorded those
> coordinates, and they land on it."

`URANUS_RING_*` and the 97.77 degree obliquity are untouched by this revision;
that is what the 7.77 degrees is.

## Twenty-nine findings, and what measurement did to them

Final tally: **0 BLOCKING, 9 NON-BLOCKING, 7 RENDERER, 13 WITHDRAWN.**

Thirteen withdrawals are a lot, and twelve of them share one cause that the
critic diagnosed itself rather than being told:

> "I read an orthographic isometric projection as if it had depth, and turned
> projection overlaps into collisions, cantilevers, off-grid placement and a
> crowded board."

Every one was answered by measuring rather than by arguing:

- **Bases overlapping, a three-piece pile-up, pieces off the cell grid.** Every
  base is Ø34.00 mm on a 36.00 mm cell pitch; in the opening position the two
  closest piece centres are **50.91 mm apart, which is 16.91 mm of clear air**.
  The critic then noticed something the Manager had not pointed out: "M2's
  50.91 mm closest spacing is 36 x sqrt(2) to five significant figures -- a
  clean diagonal on a 36 mm pitch. The pieces are on cell centres."
- **A ring overhanging the board edge, another cantilevered past the rim.** The
  board spans X -134.00 to +134.00 and Y -170.00 to +170.00 mm; every ring
  occurrence lies inside it, the flat rings reaching X 94.29-121.71 and the
  upright hoops X 106.27-110.12. Both withdrawn.
- **A prominence intersecting a globe**, twice. The critic declined to drop
  these on projection alone and asked for one number instead: minimum
  flame-to-globe clearance with a piece in an adjacent cell.
  `measure/seated-piece-clearance.md` answers it on the solids at the two real
  standing heights -- 6.00 mm in a corona well, 11.20 mm on a star --
  **0.000000 mm3 shared, sixteen of sixteen, largest globe included.** Closed.
- **Tray pockets running into each other with no dividing wall.** The critic
  refused to drop this one on the wall gate alone, and was right to: "a pocket
  that does not **locate and retain** a puck is a function problem, not a print
  problem, and nothing above addresses it." The numbers: pockets are Ø34.40 on a
  38.00 mm pitch, so **3.60 mm of solid tray wall between neighbours**, 3.50 mm
  deep with 2.50 mm of floor under them, receiving a Ø34.00 x 5.00 mm base with
  0.20 mm of clearance per side and 1.50 mm standing proud. Withdrawn: "I read
  the absence of a separating shadow as the absence of a separating wall."
- **A claimed inversion in the size ladder.** Measured in the pixels of the
  exact frames the critic was shown, colour-segmented at full resolution: 223,
  215, 183, 182, 139, 135, 123 and 103 px against 26.97, 26.00, 22.02, 21.89,
  16.70, 16.53, 14.72 and 13.78 mm -- 8.27 to 8.36 px/mm, constant, monotonic,
  no inversion. The critic withdrew and reconstructed its own error to the
  pixel, then said why it should not have made the claim: "my method carried an
  error band of at least 5-8 per cent per edge on a downscaled render, which is
  larger than the effect I claimed to detect."
- **Two "blown-out specular blobs" on Saturn.** Both were Saturn's white polar
  cap. The critic found the proof itself: "the cream and yellow bands run as
  concentric arcs **around** that white oval ... Latitude bands do exactly that
  when the pole tips toward the camera."

Seven findings are properties of `render_review`, which shades flat with no
ground plane and no shadow: the black bases reading as ink puddles, the trays
appearing to float, and the crowded look of the isometric opening position. The
critic accepted these in full and undertook not to "smuggle it back as a
real-world claim".

## The nine findings that stand

None is blocking and none is in this revision's scope, and they are listed so
they are not lost.

1. **The ringed world is the widest OBJECT in the set**, ahead of the largest
   globe -- 250 px against 224 in the ladder frame, from a Ø30.00 mm ring
   against a Ø26.97 mm globe. "A player does not see globes; a player sees
   pieces. At the table, the biggest thing on the board is not the top-ranked
   piece." Saturn's ring is frozen by this Wish and by every Wish in this chain.
2. **Two of the ladder's seven steps are below the threshold of the eye** --
   0.13 mm between Uranus and Neptune, 0.17 mm between Earth and Venus. The
   critic flagged that its own sign-off on the ladder covers five steps and that
   the other two pass on the measurement's authority, not its own.
3. **The hoop's feet read as a snapped-off end.** Now known to be deliberate and
   uniform across every ring in the set, and structurally sound. "A correct
   joint that does not look intentional."
4. **The white ring against the cream globe and the white base** have almost no
   value separation in one frame. Not measured by this run's ring-tone report,
   which measures white against cyan.
5. **Saturn's polar cap loses its boundary** at pole-toward-camera angles on
   this shader and reads as a highlight.
6. **The base numerals are illegible** at any distance. The critic escalated
   rather than dropped this one; see the largest risk below.
7. **Jupiter's red spot sits low enough at its mounting rotation** to be half
   hidden by the collar, and the same family of note applies to Neptune's dark
   spot on one army.
8. **Mars's pale patches read as irregular blobs** rather than as features.
9. **The isometric camera stacks pieces along the projection axis**, so the
   opening position reads as crowded. Worth a different camera for any published
   image.

## The signature question, split

Asked in round 2 whether the set's central idea arrives from the pictures alone,
the critic's first answer was "two-thirds of an idea arrives unaided; the
load-bearing third does not." That was taken seriously rather than argued with,
and the question was split -- with "not unmistakable" named explicitly as a
usable and expected answer for either half.

**(a) THE EXPERIENCE -- a two-player board game played with the planets; the
pieces ARE planets; they are different sizes in a way that looks deliberate and
ordered; the two sides are the same planets facing each other: UNMISTAKABLE.**
All four clauses are in the blind transcript. But the critic attached a caveat
and asked for it to travel with the answer, so it does:

> "the fourth -- *different sizes in a way that looks deliberate and ordered
> rather than assorted* -- **arrives from images 3 and 4 and does not arrive
> from image 1.**"
>
> Round 1, on the board frame: "I read image 1 as 'assorted planets of varying
> sizes' -- I did not read 'ranked'."

**(b) THE DERIVATION -- that each globe is the fifth root of the real diameter,
and that this ordering IS the strength ranking: NOT UNMISTAKABLE.** Emphatically,
in both halves, and the critic showed its working:

> "Here is how close I actually got, quoted exactly from round 1: *'the largest
> ball is about **twice** the diameter of the smallest.'* That observation is
> correct -- 26.97 / 13.78 = 1.96 -- and it is a *perfect* fingerprint of the
> rule, because the real diameter ratio is about 28.6 and 28.6^(1/5) = 1.95. I
> had the number in my hands and it told me nothing ... **The rule is elegant,
> it is real, and it is invisible.** I would call it a designer's satisfaction
> rather than a player's."

And the half that costs something, now that it knew the game:

> "**In Jungle Chess, rank is the entire mechanic.** Every capture is a rank
> comparison ... the *ranking itself* is not reliably readable during play."

With one mitigation it volunteered against its own case:

> "**'bigger beats smaller' is an intuition players import for free.** ... So
> the ranking is *guessable* even though it is not *derivable*, and the guess
> works. That covers five of the seven steps."

## Desirability

Round 1: "**Yes -- the idea is desirable. This particular execution is not
yet.**" Round 3, after thirteen of its own findings had been withdrawn on
measurement and asked for the yes or the no on its own, with the qualifier
stated separately: **"FINISHED PRODUCT DESIRABLE -- YES."**

> "Strip those out and what remains is a set that **passes all 24 print gates on
> all 24 geometries**, has a monotonic ladder, has genuinely mirrored armies,
> and has a terrain system that -- now that I can name it -- is a clean mapping
> of Dou Shou Qi's river, traps and den onto an asteroid belt, two coronas with
> prominences, and an anti-sol core. That mapping is good. It is the kind of
> thematic fit that is hard to do and easy to botch, and it wasn't botched."

On the blank globe specifically, which was one of its three round-1 reasons for
withholding:

> "I now soften [it]. It is one piece in sixteen; it is a taste call the owner
> made deliberately, having looked at the finished object, which is the right way
> to make a taste call; and R1 confirms it was executed cleanly with nothing left
> behind. I still think the set reads better with a marking there ... but it does
> not make the product undesirable. It makes one piece quieter than its
> neighbours."

## The largest risk, in the critic's own words

> "**That it ends up owned rather than played.** Every part of this object is
> finished to a standard that makes people want it on a shelf -- and rank, the
> one thing the game consists of, is the one thing the pieces will not tell you
> across a table. Two of the seven steps are a tenth of a millimetre, and the
> numeral that should rescue them cannot be read. The risk is not that someone
> dislikes it. It is that someone loves it, sets it up, gets four moves in, has
> to pick two pieces up and hold them together to find out which one wins, and
> quietly decides it is an ornament."

Neither half is in this revision's scope. The ladder is frozen by this Wish and
the numeral was not touched. It is recorded rather than answered.

## The quote check

The Wish required that any quote a critic attributes to its own earlier round be
checked against the transcript before sealing, in either direction, because the
first attempt at this brief caught a critic fabricating a self-quote that
favoured it.

**That check was run, on every self-quote in rounds 2 and 3, and all of them
pass.** Nineteen quotations in round 2 and five in round 3 are verbatim from the
round they are attributed to, or correctly elided with an ellipsis. Nothing was
invented in either direction.

The check also caught the opposite of a fabrication, which is worth recording.
In round 2 the critic used a phrase -- "the 0.6 per cent the rule predicts and
which you correctly said no reader can adjudicate by eye" -- and explicitly
declined to claim it as its own: "that is your phrasing of my point". It was the
Manager's phrasing, from the round-2 prompt, and it does not appear in round 1.
The critic's attribution was exactly right.

## What is NOT claimed here

**Geometry is frozen as reviewed: no source that builds geometry changed after
round 1.** The one source edit made during the review was prose -- the
ring-stance wording in `measure/world_separation.py`, corrected on the critic's
own finding -- and it moved no solid, no STEP and no image.

Print status is sealed `digitally-verified-not-print-ready` with
`print_ready_claim: false` and an empty `print_gate_sha256s` map, so nothing here
is offered as a print-readiness claim, even though every printable part cleared
the 0.80 mm wall gate and the 45 degree overhang gate at a 0.4 mm nozzle in its
own round in this run.

Motion is unverified, never passed: the run-root `MAKE-OPTIONS.json` sets
`check_motion` false, and nothing in this set moves against anything else in any
case.

Nothing here has been printed, handled or played. A blind reading of renders is
evidence about pictures of an object, not about the object in a hand.
