# Mercury's corrected surface, measured on the built solids

Sampled 0.10 mm under the sphere, in the planet's own frame, carried
into the piece by the same obliquity the part uses, and classified
against every colour body of the world. `#` is the `cocoa_brown` the
plains and the Caloris rim share, `O` is the `white` Caloris floor,
`.` is the bare `gray` globe, and a blank is off the piece -- the
globe is sunk 2.00 mm into its disc and cut at the disc's top face,
so the bottom of the ball does not exist.

One degree of arc is 0.1203 mm on this Ø13.78 globe and the nozzle is
0.40 mm, so a printable feature has to hold 3.33 degrees.

## Do the plains fuse?

The `cocoa_brown` cells above, flooded four-ways with the longitude
seam joined, and each group named by the ring centres that fall
inside it. Caloris's rim is `cocoa_brown` too and is expected to
come out as a group of its own.

| group | cells | rings inside it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 298 | plain_equatorial, plain_north_west |
| 2 | 258 | plain_south_lead, plain_south_mid, plain_trailing |
| 3 | 194 | plain_high_north |
| 4 | 114 | plain_far_side |
| 5 | 87 | caloris_rim |

Both expected groups came out fused: plain_south_lead + plain_south_mid + plain_trailing; and plain_north_west + plain_equatorial.

20 of the rows that cross the floor carry rim on both sides of it.
The narrowest rim any of them measures is 0.74 mm of arc, at
latitude +28.

The rim is `cocoa_brown`, the same filament as the plains, so on the
scan it shares their glyph. On the piece it is unmistakable because
it encloses a `white` floor and nothing else on this globe does.

## Is any plain still a circle?

A disc of area A has width 2*sqrt(A/pi) at its middle and the same
width whichever way it is measured. For each group of fused plains
the table gives the cells it covers, the width of its widest row and
of its widest column, and the ratio between them. A circle scores
1.00 both ways; anything lobed does not.

| group | cells | widest row | widest column | row/column |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 298 | 23 | 16 | 1.44 |
| 2 | 258 | 23 | 13 | 1.77 |
| 3 | 194 | 17 | 15 | 1.13 |
| 4 | 114 | 12 | 13 | 0.92 |
| 5 | 87 | 12 | 9 | 1.33 |

Read this as a smell test rather than as a gate: a lobed region can
still score near 1.00 by accident, and the picture above is the
evidence that matters. What it does establish is that no group here
is the equal-width disc the round-patch build produced.

## Is any bare gray enclosed and too thin to print?

The gray cells, flooded in two dimensions with the longitude seam
joined, to separate a gray island the nozzle would have to lay down
inside the brown from a gray bay open to the rest of the planet,
which it would not.

**The bare gray is one connected region.** Nothing on this globe
asks the printer for a dot of gray surrounded by brown.

The white Caloris floor is enclosed by the rim by design, so it is
not counted here; its boundary is measured in
`measure/mercury-atlas-resolution.md` and again in the basin scan
above.

## Are the two armies still mirrors?

The same grid classified on the Anti-Sol piece and compared cell by
cell. Mercury's obliquity is 0.03 degrees, so the mirrored lean
moves the pattern by 0.06 degrees of arc between the two armies --
0.0072 mm, a fortieth of a nozzle width -- and the surfaces are the
same map. What is mirrored is the lean itself and the disc, which
this scan does not sample.

0 of 4920 sampled points differ between the two armies (0.00%).

## Verdict

The plains fuse where they were drawn to fuse, Caloris reads as a
rim enclosing a floor rather than as a painted dot, no group is an
equal-width disc, no enclosed gray island exists anywhere on the
globe, and the two armies carry the same surface.

Measured by `measure/mercury_surface_scan.py` on the exact solids
`parts/world.py` builds.
