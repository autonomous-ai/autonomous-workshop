# Rainward Sun

Backgammon — coronal-rain theme: rival streams of plasma drops crowd magnetic lanes, scatter lone drops, and rain back into the Sun.

This set plays ordinary two-player Backgammon as described in the 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica. Its opening choices, touch-move laws and series starts differ from modern tournament practice. The Sun, its exposed flare and the drops interpret cooling plasma moving along solar magnetic structures. This is an astronomical analogy, not a simulation of plasma physics.

## Your set

There are 35 printed pieces: one 190 ×190 ×28 mm Sun, 15 single-tail drops, 15 fork-tail drops, two 16 mm dice and two open dice cups. Each drop is 12 ×8 ×4 mm. The two tail shapes identify the players even in one color. No doubling cube is used.

The Sun has 24 open lanes in four banks of six. Its raised central flare is the **bar**, where hit drops wait to re-enter. Drops removed from play sit on the table beside their owner's home bank. Each lane holds five drops in a row and three layers, so a point can hold all 15 of one player's drops. Stacking changes only how the pieces fit, never their rules.

## Finish the dice before playing

The printed dice are intentionally plain, solid cubes. Print each at 100% infill in one homogeneous material. Mark its faces using a fine permanent ink pen, following the pip guide below. Use a thin ink layer, not stickers, thick paint, drilled pits or raised dots. Let the ink dry before placing the dice in the cups.

Opposite faces must be **1 and 6**, **2 and 5**, and **3 and 4**. Mark 1 on any face, 6 opposite it; then 2 on a remaining face and 5 opposite it; put 3 and 4 on the last opposite pair. Both cubes receive every value once.

Ink only the filled circles; the small dots show empty positions and are not marks. The different orientations of a valid pip pattern have the same value. Inspect the printed cubes for equal dimensions, flat faces and defects before use; reject visibly distorted dice. Symmetric CAD geometry preserves the intended unbiased design, but actual printed dice have not undergone a physical fairness test. Roll the pair from your cup onto a clear, flat part of the table, away from the occupied Sun. If either die does not lie squarely, reroll both.

## Orient the Sun and set up

Use the [point-orientation diagram](orientation.svg). It is a top-view teaching map; the physical lanes are unnumbered. The taller flare crests belong at the top of the diagram, away from you. The first lane counterclockwise after the wide lower separator is point 1. The four wider separators mark the boundaries before points 1,7,13 and19. Each bank contains six lanes. Identify those boundaries first, then count within a bank.

The single-tail player is **A** and reads the diagram's point numbers directly. A moves 24→23→…→1 and then off, clockwise around the Sun. The fork-tail player is **B** and travels the other way: diagram 1→2→…→24 and then off. For B, their own point number is 25 minus the diagram number. A's home is diagram 1–6; B's home is diagram 19–24. Each player's own points 1–6 form their home bank.

Place each player's 15 drops as follows:

| Player | Diagram point | Number of drops |
|---|---:|---:|
|A: single-tail|24|2|
|A: single-tail|13|5|
|A: single-tail|8|3|
|A: single-tail|6|5|
|B: fork-tail|1|2|
|B: fork-tail|12|5|
|B: fork-tail|17|3|
|B: fork-tail|19|5|

Equivalently, each player puts 2, 5, 3, 5 drops on their **own** points 24,13,8,6. Keep tails toward the center and broad heads toward the rim. Fill rows from the rim inward. Leave the flare and both bearing-off areas empty. Each player takes one cup; both players share the same pair of dice.

## Choose the first player

The historical account allows an opening contest with one die per player **or** two dice per player. Use the same contest method for both people. With one die each, the higher face wins first play. That player may use the pair just cast as the opening throw, or roll both dice again. With two dice each, compare totals; the higher total wins first play and may use their own pair or roll both again. Repeat tied contests until one player has the higher result.

The two-die comparison and tied-contest explanation make explicit the ordinary reading of the historical account's abbreviated opening description. Do not replace its choice to keep or reroll with a compulsory modern opening roll. The source notes at the end distinguish these explanatory readings from expressly stated rules.

## Take a turn

After the opening, alternate turns rolling both dice. Each die permits a move of exactly that many points toward your home and exit. Move two different drops, or move one drop in two stages. Every stage must have a legal landing. A pair of equal dice gives four moves of that number; divide them among your drops in any legal way.

Use the entire throw whenever a legal sequence permits it. Choose the order that allows both dice to be used; you cannot deliberately play one first to block yourself from using the other. If only part of the throw is playable, use every playable portion. Unusable portions are lost. The1911 account does not impose the modern higher-die priority when either of two values, but not both, could be played; that priority is not added here.

A point holding two or more opposing drops is blocked. You may land on an empty point, a point holding your own drops, or a point containing exactly one opposing drop. In the last case, **hit** that lone drop: lift it onto the central flare and put yours on its former point. A dense pair is protected; an isolated drop can be reheated and displaced.

## Enter from the flare first

If any of your drops is on the flare, enter those drops before moving any of your other drops. A die 1 enters your own point 24, a 2 enters 23, and so on through a 6 entering 19. This is the opponent's home bank, counting from their point 1. Entry points obey the same blocking and hitting rules as any other landing.

Once your last barred drop has entered, use any legal remainder of the throw normally. When the dice do not allow entry because their destinations are blocked, lose those unusable portions. Keep every hit drop visible on the flare. It is never removed permanently by a hit.

## Bear off and win

You may begin bearing off only when all your remaining drops are in your own home points 1–6. A die can remove one drop from its matching point onto the table beside your home. You may make a legal internal move instead of bearing off.

If the matching point is empty and one of your drops is farther from the exit, use the die for a legal internal move. If all your occupied points are below the die's value, remove a drop from your highest occupied point. Continue to use the whole legal throw. If you are hit after beginning to bear off, enter that drop and bring it home before bearing any more.

The first player to remove all 15 drops wins immediately. The result measures the other stream's remaining drainage deficit:

| Result | Other player's position when the winner finishes | Score |
|---|---|---:|
|Single|At least one opposing drop has already been borne off.|1|
|Gammon|No opposing drop has been borne off, and no opposing drop is on the flare or in the winner's home.|2|
|Backgammon|No opposing drop has been borne off, and at least one is on the flare or in the winner's home.|3|

For a series, restore the complete starting setup after each game. After a single, its winner rolls both dice first in the next game. After a gammon, each player casts one die to decide first play, repeating ties; the higher caster may keep the pair or roll both again. The same contested restart after a backgammon follows the period-supported reading described below. No target series length, doubling rule, extra tie-breaker or money stake is imposed by this set.

## Keep the historical play laws

1. If the person rolling touches a drop that can be played, that drop must be played when possible. Touching an unplayable drop carries no penalty. Apply this together with the whole-throw rule.
2. A move is completed when the drop is placed on a point and released.
3. Accidentally omitting a drop carries no penalty. This does not reduce the 15-drop setup or grant a scoring advantage.
4. Drops borne off before a hit drop has been entered must be entered again. Restore those prematurely removed drops; do not keep them as successfully borne off.
5. Once the opponent has rolled following an incorrectly played throw, change that completed mistaken play only if both people agree.
6. If either die is cocked rather than resting squarely, make a fresh throw of both dice.

No modern tournament correction procedure, clock, doubling cube, special solar power or extra turn is added.

## The pictured hit

The before/after scene is one legal six-point submove, not a second game mode. It can follow this sequence from the standard setup:

1. A uses an opening 6,1 to play their 13→7 and 8→7.
2. B rolls 6,1 and plays their 13→7 and 24→23. This leaves a lone fork-tail on diagram point 1.
3. A rolls 6,2 and first moves 7→1 using the 6. The attacker replaces the lone fork-tail at the near rim; that fork-tail is lifted to the exposed flare, 80 mm away in the model. The 2 remains to play; for example, A can then move 8→6.

The droplets' cooling, crowding and reheating explain the physical story. The competitive blocking and score are still the unchanged board-game rules.

## Making, keeping and understanding the set

Print the Sun and drops on their flat undersides, and the cups open-side up. The two cups have 42 mm outside diameter, 38 mm inside diameter and 28 mm height, with 2 mm walls and floors. Their interiors accommodate the shared 16 mm dice pair. No support structures, fasteners or assembly hardware form part of the design. Consult the included manufacturing verification records for the particular CAD revision; no successful physical print, tactile fit, durability or human play session is claimed.

Keep drops loose and visible during play. Stack identical silhouettes directly, with tails aligned. Store pieces in a separate container when finished; the two open cups are dice cups and are not claimed to hold the entire set. Small pieces make this a game set for people able to handle dice and counters appropriately.

## Historical and astronomical sources

The frozen source is the ordinary game in [Encyclopaedia Britannica, 1911](https://bkgm.com/articles/Britannica/Backgammon/index.html); its separate Russian Backgammon rules are excluded. [Penn's Online Books Page](https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/work?id=olbp16644) identifies the 1911 edition as fully in the US public domain. This guide and the physical design are original expression; no publisher's artwork, rulebook wording or branding is reproduced.

Britannica abbreviates opening ties, comparison of two-die opening throws and the next start after a triple backgammon. Tied contests and contested starts after both gammon and backgammon are corroborated by the period text [Hoyle's Games Modernized, 1909](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/39445/39445-h/39445-h.htm). Comparing two-die totals and retaining the winning pair are stated here as conventional interpretations of Britannica's one-or-two-dice option. That other book's different option of quadruple scoring is **not** used: this set preserves Britannica's 1/2/3 results.

[NASA's account of coronal rain](https://www.nasa.gov/solar-system/unexpected-rain-on-sun-links-two-solar-mysteries/) describes plasma cooling and descending along magnetic structures, with reconnection providing a possible change of path. The set makes an unusual original combination of this phenomenon with historical Backgammon; it does not claim that a circular board itself is new or that no similar theme exists.
