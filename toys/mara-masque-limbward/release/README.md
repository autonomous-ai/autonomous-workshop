# Limbward

Chess — coronagraph theme: rival optical masks cross a focal-plane board to leave the opposing star no safe line of sight.

Limbward plays standard two-player chess. Its camera-shaped board represents a sampled focal plane, and its pieces represent a star and the masks used to hide starlight. This is an optical metaphor: illumination, shadows, the direction a piece faces and literal lines of sight never decide a move. No light source is required.

## In the set

There are 97 individual items: one camera-board, 32 starting pieces and 64 spare promotion pieces. Each side has its normal starting army plus eight spare queens, eight spare rooks, eight spare bishops and eight spare knights. Leave all promotion spares off the board until needed. They are replacements, never pieces that can be dropped into play.

White has smooth circular feet. Black has three scallops around each foot. Colour provides another cue, but the feet identify the sides without it. Six shapes identify the roles:

| Chess role | Limbward shape | Ordinary movement |
|---|---|---|
| King | Rounded stellar dome | One square in any direction, subject to safety; also castling. |
| Queen | Tall annular mask with a circular well | Any unobstructed distance along a rank, file or diagonal. |
| Rook | Tall, flat-topped cross of vanes | Any unobstructed distance along a rank or file. |
| Bishop | Shorter diagonal vanes with a peaked crest | Any unobstructed distance along a diagonal. |
| Knight | Asymmetric stepped baffle | Two squares along a rank or file and one square perpendicular to it. |
| Pawn | Low, blunt knife-edge wedge | Advances straight forward; captures diagonally forward. |

A rank runs across the board; a file runs between the players. “Forward” means toward the opponent's starting edge. Rotating a sculpture does not change its role or its forward direction.

## Set up

Place the board between the players with a light, smooth corner square on each player's right. The alternating recessed borders mark the dark squares. The board has eight ranks and eight files, with 20 mm square spacing.

White's back rank, from left to right, is rook, knight, bishop, queen, king, bishop, knight, rook. Place eight White pawns directly in front. Black places the same arrangement from White's left-to-right viewpoint on the opposite back rank, with eight Black pawns in front. Each queen begins on a square matching her side: White queen on light, Black queen on dark. The kings face each other along one file.

In conventional coordinates, files are **a–h** from White's left to right and ranks are **1–8** from White toward Black. White's king starts on e1 and queen on d1; Black's king starts on e8 and queen on d8. The light corner nearest White's right hand is h1.

White moves first. Players then alternate one legal move at a time. All pieces and board information remain visible to both players; there is no random element.

## Moving and capturing

Lift a piece or slide it to its destination. A move cannot finish on a square occupied by your own piece. To capture an opposing piece, move onto its square and remove it from the board. En passant is the one capture exception described below. The king is never captured.

Queens, rooks and bishops cannot cross an occupied square. Knights can jump over intervening pieces. No piece may move in a way that leaves its own king attacked. The two kings therefore cannot stand on adjacent squares.

A pawn normally advances one square into an empty square on its file. From its starting square, it may instead advance two squares if both the crossed square and destination are empty. A pawn captures an opposing piece one square diagonally forward. It cannot move backward or capture straight ahead.

For ordinary touch-move play, use one hand to make a move. Announce an intention to adjust a piece before adjusting it. If you intentionally touch your own piece, you must move the first such piece that can legally move. If you intentionally touch an opposing piece, you must capture the first such piece that can legally be captured. If both sides' pieces are touched, capture the first opposing piece with the first own piece touched when legal; otherwise the ordinary touch-move obligations still apply. Accidental contact does not establish that intention. If no touched piece can be legally moved or captured, choose any legal move.

Releasing a piece on its legal destination commits that move. For a capture, remove the captured piece; for promotion, remove the pawn and release its replacement; for castling, complete both king and rook placement. These physical procedures do not create extra chess choices.

## Castling

Castling is one move involving your king and an original rook. The king moves two squares toward that rook, and the rook moves onto the square the king crossed.

You may castle only when:

- The king has never moved, and that rook has never moved.
- All squares between them are empty.
- The king is not in check, and neither the square it crosses nor its destination is attacked.

A king or rook that returns to its starting square does not regain lost castling rights. An attacked rook can still castle when all the king's conditions are satisfied. On the queen's side, an attack on the square between the rook and the king's destination does not prohibit castling; that square must still be empty.

Move the king first. Deliberately touching the rook before the king does not allow castling on that move. If you touch the king and then a rook intending to castle but that castle is illegal, make another legal king move if one exists, which may be a legal castle on the other side. If the king has no legal move, you may make another legal move.

The theme describes castling as sheltering the star alongside a vane. It grants no additional protection beyond the ordinary chess position.

## En passant

Immediately after an opposing pawn advances two squares from its starting square and finishes beside your pawn on an adjacent file, your pawn may capture it as though it had advanced only one square. Move your pawn diagonally onto the square the opposing pawn crossed, and remove that opposing pawn from the square where it actually stands.

This opportunity lasts only for your immediately following move. It is legal only if your king remains safe. In the theme, this is interception of a passing knife-edge; it introduces no new movement rule.

## Promotion

When your pawn reaches the furthest rank, replace it immediately, as part of that same move, with a queen, rook, bishop or knight of your side. The new piece has its full ordinary powers immediately, including the ability to give check or mate. If the move captures a piece on that final square, remove the captured piece too.

You may choose any of the four roles, regardless of which pieces have previously been captured. You cannot keep the pawn or promote to a king. Use the matching spare promotion piece; remove the pawn from play. The choice is fixed when the replacement touches the promotion square. The pawn may be removed before or after placing its replacement.

The supplied eight replacements of each role per side preserve every promotion choice even if all eight pawns choose the same role. Spares have exactly the same function as an existing piece of that role. There is no separate reserve action.

## Check, checkmate and draws

Your king is in **check** when attacked by an opposing piece. You must answer with a legal move that removes the threat: move the king, capture the attacker or interpose a piece when the position permits. A piece can attack a square even when moving that attacking piece would expose its own king; keep that distinction when determining king safety.

**Checkmate** means the king is in check and there is no legal reply. The checking player wins immediately. Leave the king on the board: the exposed star's occultation has become unavoidable, but the king is not taken.

**Stalemate** means the player to move has no legal move and is not in check. It is an immediate draw.

A **dead position** is an immediate draw when neither side could checkmate by any possible sequence of legal moves. This tests what is possible, not what either player expects to achieve. Do not replace it with a simple piece-count rule.

A player may **resign**, normally losing immediately. However, if the opponent cannot checkmate by any possible legal continuation, resignation produces a draw instead.

Players may **agree to a draw** after each has made at least one move. An offer does not stop play until accepted. In ordinary play, an offer persists until accepted, rejected verbally or rejected by the opponent touching a piece with the intention to move or capture; a game-ending result also ends it.

### Draws that require a claim

A correct **threefold repetition** claim draws the game when the same position has occurred at least three times. The occurrences need not be consecutive. The same player must be to move, the same kinds of pieces of the same sides must occupy the same squares, and the legal possibilities must match. Castling rights and a legally possible en-passant capture matter; the arrangement alone is insufficient.

A correct **50-move** claim draws the game after at least 50 moves by each player without a pawn move or capture. Either event restarts that count.

The player whose turn it is may claim when the qualifying position or count already exists, or before making a specified legal move that will produce it. For an intended-move claim, record that move and state the claim before moving; the indicated move is then fixed. Touching a piece with the intention to move or capture forfeits the right to claim under these provisions on that turn.

Three repetitions or 50 moves do not automatically end a game. A claim must be correct. In an organised event, use the arbiter's standard claim procedure; an incorrect claim follows the ordinary FIDE consequences, including the opponent's two-minute addition when a clock is used and continuation of play. The reskin does not create a different claim or penalty system.

### Automatic draws

The game ends automatically when the same position has occurred at least **five times**, using the same position test as repetition claims.

The game also ends automatically after **75 moves by each player** without a pawn move or capture. If the final move delivers checkmate, checkmate takes precedence over this 75-move rule.

There is no special astronomical tie-break or score. When scores are recorded, a win scores 1, a loss 0 and a draw ½ for each player.

## The illustrated rook move

The before-and-after illustration uses a legal example position with White king on a1, White rook on h1 and Black king on a8. All other pieces are off the board. It is White's turn.

White moves the rook from h1 to h8: seven squares, or 140 mm. The h-file is clear, White's king remains safe, and the rook now attacks Black's king along the eighth rank. This is **Rh8+**, a check. It is not checkmate. The same elevated view shows the tall vane move from the foreground to the far edge and remain above the camera rim.

This example is neither the normal starting position nor a prescribed puzzle. Begin an ordinary game with all 32 starting pieces in their standard positions.

## Handling and scope

Each item is a separate solid object. The camera-board has an integral lens bulge and grip; pieces stand freely on the playing surface. The camera itself measures 196 × 196 × 26 mm, with a 160 mm board. The pieces have 16 mm feet and stand 12–34 mm tall. Promotion pieces are kept beside the board; no internal storage capacity is promised.

The optical forms are original simplified sculptures. They do not function as an instrument, and their outlines do not add movement restrictions. For example, turning a cross-vane never changes a rook's legal directions.

No clock is included. If playing a timed or organised game, the chosen ordinary chess time control and applicable FIDE competition procedures remain unchanged. This set does not add a time control, an arbiter procedure or a variant. Physical printing, handling, durability and human playtesting have not been established by the digital model or its illustrations.

## Rules and astronomical background

The rules above are written in original prose from the [FIDE Laws of Chess effective 1 January 2023](https://handbook.fide.com/chapter/E012023). The source includes full competition procedures. Its rulebook prose and diagrams are not reproduced, and no affiliation or endorsement is implied.

Real coronagraph masks suppress stellar glare so that faint nearby objects can be observed; see [NASA's explanation of coronagraph systems](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/roman-space-telescope/coronagraph-systems/). Limbward translates that practice into an optical metaphor for unchanged chess.
