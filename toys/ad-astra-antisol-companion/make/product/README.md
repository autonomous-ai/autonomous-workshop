# Antisol Companion

Jungle Chess played with the solar system, in two armies of eight worlds.

A planet's size on the board is the fifth root of its real measured diameter,
so rank is something you can see rather than something you have to learn:
Mercury is the smallest world in play and Jupiter the largest. The traditional
rules are followed exactly as written. Matter faces antimatter -- one army's
worlds lean their poles one way and the other army's lean the other, by each
planet's own true axial tilt, and the bases say the same thing again in white
against black.

Four of the eight worlds wear a real map: Earth its own coastlines, Mars its
own dark continents, Mercury its own smooth plains with the Caloris basin in
them, and Venus -- alone in the set -- a surface no eye has ever seen. Three
more wear their weather. One wears nothing at all.

**This edition changes one thing, on one world.** Neptune's Great Dark Spot
gets the bright companion cloud that sits just south of it, on both armies:
one small white oval, ten degrees of arc long and five tall, on the spot's own
meridian. The spot stops being a lone smudge on a blue ball and becomes a
weather system with something happening beside it.

It is a feature this box knew it was missing. An earlier edition drew a
companion, a later one reverted the cloud marking it lived inside and took the
companion with it without meaning to, and the design contract recorded the loss
in as many words: *the reference does show a bright cloud beside the dark spot
and this build does not draw it.* That entry called it a live recommendation
rather than a settled question. This edition settles it.

**This time it is drawn as its own thing.** The companion has its own marking
key rather than being one more body inside Neptune's white cloud marking, which
is exactly how it was lost the first time. A later argument about Neptune's
bands cannot take it with them again.

## What it costs the dark spot, said plainly

The Great Dark Spot is fourteen degrees of arc tall, so its southern rim is
already seven degrees below its own centre. A cloud five degrees tall centred
eight degrees south of that centre -- which is where it goes -- overlaps the
rim by one and a half degrees. **There is nowhere else on that globe to put
it.** Moving it far enough south to leave a clear strip of blue between the two
takes it into the collar the globe sits on, where a third of it disappears;
that version was built and rendered and thrown away. The window between the
spot's rim and the collar is 0.46 mm, and nothing plus two printable gaps fits
in 0.46 mm.

So the spot gives way. **It is cut back not to the cloud itself but to a
keep-out one nozzle width outside it**, so a strip of bare blue survives
between them all the way round -- 0.42 mm of it at the closest point. That
matters more than it sounds. The first build cut the spot back exactly to the
cloud's edge, the two colours touched, and an independent reader shown the
pictures cold called the pair *a notched figure-eight* and the cloud *a small
grey circle* -- a lump budding off the spot rather than a cloud beside it. The
keep-out is what makes it read as its own cloud, and the same reader confirmed
it does.

**What that costs is a bay in the dark spot's southern rim**, about fifteen
degrees of arc wide and three and a half deep, on an oval twenty-eight wide and
fourteen tall. Said the way the reader said it: at the size you will actually
hold, it reads as one oval with a bite taken out of it, and as two cloud
features nested against each other -- which is what it is. Magnified five times
it reads as a comma on one army and a crescent on the other. Anyone comparing
this edition with the last one side by side will see it.

The spot itself did not move. Its outline, its latitude, its longitude and its
forty vertices are the same bytes they were, and its own shape still measures
the exact volume the last edition sealed, to a ten-thousandth of a cubic
millimetre. What changed is where the dark stops.

## What arrives

Forty-two printed parts: sixteen worlds, a board in four panels, twelve
asteroid-belt tiles, two stars, six corona cells and two storage trays. Nothing
is glued, screwed or assembled; the tiles and stars drop into their own pockets
and the panels butt together. **This edition adds no part.** A marking in this
set is a flush colour inlay -- it partitions the globe's volume without moving
the printed solid's own surface -- so both Neptune pieces come out of the
generator byte-for-byte identical to the last edition's, and the change lives
entirely in which spool prints which part of them.

## Where the eight surfaces come from

- **Mercury** -- outline. Seven smooth albedo plains traced as rings, plus the
  Caloris basin as a bright floor inside a raised rim.
- **Venus** -- outline. Seven radar highland provinces led by Aphrodite Terra,
  and three lowland plains, from the Magellan mosaic.
- **Earth** -- outline. Coastlines, dry interiors and a northern ice cap.
- **Mars** -- outline plus cap. The classical albedo map, and two polar caps
  whose rims are broken by lobes so they read as ice rather than as lids.
- **Jupiter** -- band, blob and outline. Six belts at Jupiter's own unequal
  latitudes; five bright cream zones between and beyond them; and the Great Red
  Spot as one oval with a darker collar, the widest belt bending north round it.
- **Saturn** -- band, cap and **ring**. Five bands of unequal width, unevenly
  spaced and not symmetric about the equator; a bright cap above fifty-eight
  degrees north; and a flat white ring lying almost level in Saturn's own
  equatorial plane.
- **Uranus** -- **nothing, and a ring.** One undivided cyan sphere, the only
  bare world in the box, with a white hoop standing upright over it because a
  ring lies in its planet's equatorial plane and this planet lies on its side.
- **Neptune** -- band and outline. Three closed white bands of latitude, five,
  four and three degrees of arc wide, each running the whole way round the
  planet; the Great Dark Spot as one outline oval twice as wide as it is tall;
  and **new in this edition, the spot's bright companion cloud**, one white
  oval ten degrees by five nested into the spot's southern rim at the same
  longitude.

Seven of the eight are drawn, none of them as a union of circles. The eighth is
bare on purpose.

## What the blind reader said, including the part that stopped the build

One independent reviewer, two rounds, given nothing but six pictures of this
set and forbidden to open a single file of documentation.

**Round one was cold.** It had no idea what the object was. It described
*"a small standing playing piece: a ball on a round plinth, like a chess pawn
whose head is a planet"*, named all eight worlds from their markings alone, and
when asked what it would still remember a week later answered with the thing
this whole set is built on, before anyone told it a fifth root was involved:

> The size ladder itself -- that the pieces are not uniform tokens but eight
> different-sized worlds on identical feet, so the board has a built-in
> hierarchy you can read at a glance from across the table.

**And it stopped the build.** Looking at the two Neptune close-ups, it saw the
dark spot and, touching it, *"a small grey circle"*; it called the pair *"a
notched figure-eight"* and, once told what the edition was for, said so
plainly: the one thing this edition adds did not read as a bright cloud beside
the spot, it read as a lump growing off it. It called that blocking. It was
right, and the geometry was changed rather than the wording: the keep-out
above is that repair.

**Round two read the re-rendered pictures and cleared it.** *"Separate. This is
the question my blocking finding turned on, and it is resolved... I can no
longer produce the round-one read of 'one two-lobed object' even trying to."*
It checked by machine as well as by eye: of the pixels immediately surrounding
the cloud, none are marking -- it is ringed by blue on both pieces. Its verdict
was *"No blocking visual defect remains in this build."*

**The part that is not comfortable, kept in.** The reader also said the cloud
looks grey rather than white, and it is right about the picture. It is not
right about the plastic, and the difference is measured rather than argued: the
cloud prints in the same white spool as the cloud bands, and a controlled test
-- the same piece rendered twice at one camera with only that one region's
filament changed -- puts it 34.5 tone levels from the dark spot on one army and
52.5 on the other, against a set that already ships Saturn's bands at 9.4. What
makes it look dim is the renderer: it lights from above and draws no shadow, so
every marking on a globe's southern face comes out dark whatever spool it
prints from. The reader had already proved this to itself without knowing it --
in round one it called the same globe's southernmost white band *"the darkest
of the three bands"* while calling the two northern ones *"bright white"*.

It withdrew the blocking call on tone and asked instead for two things this
edition does not give it: product pictures lit so south-facing markings are not
in shadow, and a printed sample looked at under ordinary room light before the
question is called closed. Both are in the limitations, unmet.


## What this edition could not do

**The companion is not separated from the spot by bare globe, and it cannot
be.** It shares a boundary with it over about a third of its own rim, and over
another 0.9 mm the strip of blue between them is thinner than the printer's
0.4 mm nozzle and will not appear. That is disclosed here rather than left to
be found on a printed piece: what you will see is a white oval touching a dark
one, which is what the reference photograph shows and not what the drawing says.

**The dark spot is no longer the shape the published set sealed.** It is the
same ring at the same latitude and longitude, with the same forty vertices, but
the printed dark body has a scallop taken out of its southern rim to make room
for the cloud. Anyone comparing the two editions side by side will see it.

**The companion is short, so it has a longitude and can face away from you.**
Unlike Neptune's three cloud bands, which run the whole way round the planet
and cannot be hidden by turning it, the cloud is on one face. On the Sol army
it sits low and to the south, and at the higher of the set's two photographic
angles it is close enough to the limb that it barely reads. Turn the piece and
it is there.

**None of the reference's fine cloud texture is drawn, and none of it is
faked.** Neptune's real cirrus streaks scale to between 0.13 and 0.26 mm on
this globe, under a single nozzle width. No stippling and no fine ribs stand in
for them: this set's material rules forbid deliberate grit.

**And the things next to it that are still not drawn**: a second dark spot,
polar brightening, ring arcs, and the faint darker centre inside the dark spot
which would need a fourth filament on a globe held to three. Each is listed
with its reason in the design contract.

## Before you print

Read `product.json` for the full list of limitations. Nothing here has been
printed, handled or played. Every fit, wall thickness and overhang margin is a
measurement on the exact CAD solids and a prediction for a 0.4 mm nozzle at
0.2 mm layers.

**Motion verification is switched off for this build and is recorded as
unverified rather than passed.** Nothing in the set moves relative to anything
else in any case.

**The verification finished, and every check that could run did.** The
pipeline ran for **966.47 seconds** over eleven steps: **seven ran and passed,
three are not applicable and are recorded as not run rather than as passed, and
none was left unverified.** The geometry inspection completed **all 51 of its
checks, every one `passed`** — that covers validating every one of the 222
pieces of the assembled set for sound topology and positive volume, and
checking the whole assembly for interference, which is the only way to answer
whether any two parts occupy the same space. They do not. That is the check
this edition most needed, because it adds a marking that overlaps another one.

**One disclosure about how it got there.** The inspection has a time allowance
and this run exhausted it twice before finishing. Those two attempts produced
**no verdict and none was taken from them** — an interrupted inspection is not
a result, and both were recorded as unverified at the time. The tool resumes
measurements it has already completed rather than starting over, so the three
runs advanced 1, then 24, then all 51 of the checks, and the report sealed here
is the one in which the last of them finished. `GEOMETRY-NOTES.md` carries the
same record.

Separately, in their own isolated rounds, all 24 printed parts cleared the
0.80 mm wall gate and the 45-degree overhang gate at a 0.4 mm nozzle: 24 of 24
on each, with no region anywhere needing support and no bridges. Those are
measurements on the CAD solids and a prediction for a 0.4 mm nozzle at 0.2 mm
layers. **No print-readiness is claimed and none is sealed.**

**Both Neptune files came out byte-for-byte identical to the last edition's**,
and that is the check rather than a coincidence. A marking here is a flush
colour inlay: it partitions the globe's volume without moving the printed
solid's own surface. If this edition had cut deeper than it was meant to, or
re-cut a seat, or rebuilt a globe, those two hashes would have moved with it.
